# Chapter 3: The Passenger in the Wall

The three taps came again. Ivara took the red key from the stone basin, then answered with two, though nobody had taught her the pattern. A seam opened around the round door. Beyond it lay a strip of darkness so narrow that a person could pass only sideways.

Tovan lifted the bone comb. “We should take this to the Hall and make a proper record.”

“Then it will be classified as a pocket,” said Ivara, “and forgotten with the other pockets.”

The room behind the door tapped once. Pell held the tin box against his chest. “I know that sound.”

He went through first. On the other side, the narrow passage beyond the round door widened into a little chamber lined with paper. Every wall was covered by names in different hands. The names had been crossed out so many times that some were only grooves. At the center sat a child-sized chair, and on the chair lay a chalk moth. It was flat and white, with two wings shaped like spoons.

“I left that,” Pell said. “I was small.”

Ivara looked at him. “Eren Vey said he remembered the song, but not the place.”

“That was Eren Vey. I said I didn't remember the place.”

There was a difference, and the red key stayed cool. Pell had told the truth, though not the whole truth.

Eren Vey turned the brass map toward the chalk moth. The line between the Hall and the Orchard moved beneath his thumb. A new line appeared, tracing the narrow chamber and then continuing through the wall. “This is not a map of places,” he said. “It's a map of who can remember them.”

The names on the wall were those of children born in rooms that had gone missing. Some had become clerks, some porters, some simply disappeared from the census. Ivara found her own name cut into the paper, though she had no memory of entering the chamber. Beside it was a second name: Alis Noll.

The old ledger said Alis had been buried in the family niche. The Hall's register listed her as “unplaced at birth.” Ivara had assumed the phrase meant her sister had not taken a first breath.

The chalk moth lifted one wing. Beneath it lay a numbered spool, the seventh tooth from the comb tied around its middle. Tovan lunged. Ivara caught his wrist.

“You knew Alis,” she said.

“I knew her file,” he answered.

The red key warmed. This was another practiced answer. Tovan had known the child.

He took the key from Ivara and pressed it to the chalk moth. The flat creature opened its wings. A small voice came out of it, the note from the Mouth Room, followed by a girl's whisper: “Don't let them divide the first house.”

Tovan went pale. Eren Vey stared at him. Ivara turned to Tovan. “Who was Alis to me?”

“Alis,” Tovan said. “She was not your sister by blood. She was your sister by address. Mara brought her home through the Mouth Room and into the Latch.”

The correction struck like a dropped stone. Ivara had believed Alis dead and buried. She had not been buried. Tovan had helped hide her in the first house, before rooms had separate names. The Partition divided the first house into the Vellum Hall and the Tin Orchard so each could be governed. Its hidden rooms endured between them. When the division began to fail, it cut the names of every person born between the parts.

Pell had been a child in that dividing house. He remembered Alis as a friend, and the wall remembered them both. He touched the paper beside her name. The grooves filled with ink, letter by letter.

Tovan gave Ivara back the red key. “Your mother asked me to keep the girl hidden. She said the Latch would be safest if no one could find it.”

“Then why did you say it was sealed?”

“Because it was. I believed the Latch could not open again.”

That belief had lasted for years. It fell now, though Tovan kept his eyes on the wall.

The numbered spool rolled from beneath the moth and stopped at Eren Vey's boot. He picked it up, unwound a short length, and listened. He heard a name nobody else could hear. He did not tell them what it was. Instead, he put the spool in his coat and said, “We need to go to the Nacre Stair.”

Ivara asked why.

“Because someone is pulling the other end.”

They left the chamber. When they turned back, the round door had become an unmarked panel. Pell carried the tin box, Ivara the thimble, Tovan the comb, Eren the map and spool. The chalk moth remained in the chair.

At the Understair threshold, Pell stopped and told Ivara that he had sent the box because her mother once said Ivara could hear a room without trying to own it. He had not known whether she would come.

The red key stayed cool in Ivara's palm. She believed him.

They studied the paper wall until the lamps in the passage began to dim. Each crossed-out name carried a small mark beside it: a circle for a room that had been joined, a square for a room that had been divided, and a line for a person who had moved away. Beside Alis's name was a small open door. Beside Ivara's was a key.

“Who wrote these?” asked Eren Vey.

“Children,” Pell said. “We marked the wall so it would remember what the grownups wanted to split.”

Pell told them he had arrived at the first house as a boy whose name could not stay in one place. One week the Orchard called him Pell; the Hall called him Aven; the stair did not call him anything. His mother brought him there because the house could hold all of him at once. He had played with Alis and Nima, and the baby Ivara had slept in a drawer padded with old bark.

“I knew the Hall had made you a porter. I didn't know you had been Pell Aven before that,” Ivara said.

“I thought that was what I was allowed to be.”

Eren Vey turned to the wall and found his own name. It had not been crossed out. The open door beside it pointed toward the Hall. He had spent a single night in the first house when he was very young, but he had not been one of the four children who named it. The room had remembered him anyway. He saw now that the note in his voice was the same note that called from the Mouth Room.

Ivara asked Tovan how he had known her mother. He said Mara Noll had worked as a clerk at the Hall before Ivara was born. She had cared for Alis before and after the Partition, and she had taken the red key when the first house became inaccessible. Mara had not been the girl's birth mother, but she had been the person who taught both sisters to call a room home.

“You made me think she was dead,” Ivara said.

“I made you think she was buried. I knew she was alive, but I did not know whether the room could keep her safe.”

The key stayed cool. This time Tovan had told the truth about what he knew; Ivara still had reason to resent his choice. The Registry treated such corrections as harmless errors. The people inside the errors had to live with them.

The chalk moth's wings opened wider. It spoke in Mara's voice: “The key goes to the youngest hand. The comb goes to the one who divided. The thread goes to the one who hears.”

Tovan heard his instruction and removed the comb from his grip. Eren took the spool from his pocket. Ivara placed the key on the chair. The chalk moth's wings folded again, and she took the key back into her hand. For one brief moment the paper wall showed the names without their crossed-out marks. Then the names faded, leaving only the grooves behind.

Nima was not there, but Ivara could hear the Orchard keeper somewhere above them, striking the branches with a washer. The three-note song traveled down through the floors. They followed it to the Understair, carrying the objects that had begun to choose their own bearers.

After leaving the hidden chamber, Ivara went to the Hall's record shelves alone. She wanted to see the winter roll where Pell Aven had disappeared. The file began with a child's name and ended with a blank space. No death had been recorded, no transfer, no dismissal. The space was clean in the way a room looks after furniture has been removed.

She found a second copy of the roll stored under the old title “Names without a door.” This version had notes in Mara's hand. Pell's line read, “The porter is not missing; the Hall is asking the wrong question.” Beneath it, the name Eren Vey appeared with a mark for temporary residence in the first house. Mara had kept the evidence, but she had not given it to Tovan. Perhaps she believed that records could be both a shield and a trap.

Ivara took neither file. She memorized the note and closed the shelf. For the first time, she understood why her mother had left the red key with her rather than in the Hall's custody. A key could be used to open something, but it could also remind its bearer not to lock a room merely because it was difficult to explain.

When she returned to the Understair, Eren Vey was sitting on the bottom step. He had unwound the numbered spool and wrapped its thread around his fingers. He said he could hear two voices in it now: the voice of the room, and the voice of the person who had carried the spool there. He did not know which one had spoken his name.

“Does it matter?” she asked.

“It matters if I pretend one voice is the other.”

He rolled the spool closed and stood. He was not ready to tell them about the night he had spent in the first house, but he had stopped believing the song belonged only to his mother. Ivara did not press him. Her own memories had begun to feel like rooms she could enter at her own pace.

Above them, the Hall's clerks were changing shifts. Their steps moved across the ceiling in careful lines. The Understair caught each one and sent it down as a different sound: a heel became a tap, a pause became a hum, a hurried footfall became a door. The building was teaching them that a single journey could be recorded in several true ways.
