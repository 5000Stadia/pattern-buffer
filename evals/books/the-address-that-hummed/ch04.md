# Chapter 4: The Stair That Counted Down

The Nacre Stair descended through the shell of Oru. Its steps were made from polished pieces of something that might once have been a giant clam, though no one in the city agreed on whether clams had ever been alive. The stair counted backward from nine hundred each time someone crossed it. It was said that if the count reached zero, the steps would become a single flat floor.

Nima waited on step 412. The brass washers on her necklace clicked with each breath. She had left the Tin Orchard without permission, and her sleeves were dusted with pale grit. In her hands she carried a folded bark strip bearing the word LATCH.

“I thought you said the room was your grandmother's,” Ivara said.

“It was the only story the Orchard gave me.” Nima ran a thumb over the strip. “I found this below the empty ring. It has my grandmother's handwriting, but it says the room was made by four children.”

Tovan asked who the other children were.

“Ivara's sister. Pell. Me.” Nima looked at her. “And you.”

“I wasn't there.”

“You were the baby in the house. They wrote your name on the wall before you could speak.”

Nima had learned what the others did not: her grandmother did not build the Latch alone. The claim that the Latch belonged to her family was a lie inherited from an older lie, and she had come to correct it. She held the bark strip out to Ivara, who put it beside the brass map. The two records aligned, though neither showed an entrance.

The stair rang below them. The count changed from 412 to 411. Someone was climbing upward.

Eren Vey tied a loose end of black thread to the numbered spool without unspooling it from the glass thimble. The two threads twitched toward one another. “This line leads to the first house.”

Tovan seized the spool. “No. It leads to the split. If you pull it, the rooms will join, and everyone inside will be moved.”

“You knew that,” said Ivara. “You hid Alis there because you knew.”

“I hid the child because there was nowhere else that would hold her name.”

Pell watched Tovan's hands. “The seventh tooth can stop the comb. You once told me it was lost.”

“I told you it was dangerous.”

The red key warmed. Tovan had lied to Pell about the tooth being lost, even though he believed the danger was real.

Nima placed a washer on the step. The stair count paused. “The Orchard has a rule. A room can be claimed only by everyone who remembers entering it.”

“That's not a rule,” said Tovan. “That's a superstition.”

“Then it won't hurt to try.”

They set the brass map on the landing. Pell opened the tin box and took out its false bottom. Under the lifted false bottom, a depression in the box held nothing now but a dark outline shaped like a key. The objects they had brought were divided among them: map to Eren Vey, thimble to Ivara, comb to Tovan, and box to Pell. The empty outline was not an absence. It was a place waiting for the red key.

Ivara set the key into the depression. The stair gave a low note and stopped counting. A rectangular outline appeared in the shell wall, then another, then another. On the lowest step, someone had scratched “ALIS NOLL” in a child's hand.

Ivara's remembered claim changed: Alis had not been buried in the family niche. She had been hidden alive in the first house. That fact now belonged to all four of them, but Eren Vey knew an additional thing. He had heard the name at the end of the spool: it was his own. As a child, he had been placed in the first house too, for a single night, and his mother took him away before the split. He had kept this from the others because he feared the Registry would decide he belonged to a room instead of himself.

He unwound the spool. His name made the black thread draw taut. The wall opened at the level of the landing, not below, and beyond it was a room with a child-sized chair, a chalk moth, and the sound of someone turning pages.

“Alis?” called Ivara.

A woman's voice answered, “Still here.”

The stair resumed its count, but now it counted upward. Nima's washers chimed. Tovan reached for the bone comb, and Pell blocked him.

“You can't make the first house hold by combing it into pieces,” Pell said. “We have to bring it back together.”

Tovan looked at the opening. “If you join it, the Hall loses its boundaries.”

“The Hall is a room,” said Ivara. “It can learn to share.”

She lifted the key from the depression in the box and gave it to Nima. Nima held the key and the bark strip. The red iron stayed cool against her palm; she had spoken truth. She had not come to claim the room. She had come to return it.

The four children named in the old record were now grown, and not all were present. Still, Ivara stepped through first.

Before they entered the opening, Ivara asked Nima why she had trusted the old story for so long. Nima told her that in the Orchard, every child received a washer from the keeper and was told the tree it came from. Her grandmother's washer had been smooth with use. When Nima was young, she had asked whether the Latch had belonged to their family. Her grandmother had answered, “It was ours to protect.” Nima had mistaken the words for “ours to own.”

The difference had shaped her whole life. She became keeper because she thought she had inherited a duty that belonged to her bloodline. Now she had learned that protection was temporary and ownership was not the same thing as care. She took the necklace from her throat and placed it on the landing. Each washer rang once, as if relieved to be off duty.

The memory of Mara Noll came to Ivara in scraps. Her mother had never said that Alis was dead; she had said Alis was “in the niche,” the phrase used in Oru for a place where a family keeps what it cannot carry. Ivara had read “in the niche” as “buried” because the Hall's register had used the word unplaced. There had been a mistake, then a correction, then another mistake built on top of it.

The stair carried them upward by degrees. A person could cross the same step twice and arrive at a different landing. Pell marked their route with small folds of paper. Eren Vey laid the black thread along the handrail. Tovan followed behind with no tool in his hand, as if he were trying to learn what a room sounded like without the comb.

At one landing, the stair opened onto the Tin Orchard. The rust-colored branches crowded the threshold. Nima recognized the empty tray where the Latch's hinge had lain. She placed the red key in the grit and waited. The ground did not open, but a nearby tree bent its roots around the metal. The key's forked tooth matched the shape of the hinge pin. It had never been a door key in the ordinary sense; it was a memory of the hinge that joined the Orchard to the first house.

Nima lifted it out. “My grandmother didn't make the room. She kept one side from falling away.”

That was the correction she would carry back to the Orchard. Her grandmother had not been the sole maker of the Latch, but she had done something important. The old story was not entirely false. It had simply made one person stand where four belonged.

They went on. When they reached the opening, Ivara saw the room's five places set for them. She realized that the table was not waiting for five people who had all lived there. It was waiting for five people who could remember it differently. The house had no use for matching memories. It needed a place where the disagreements could remain visible.

Eren Vey stopped before the threshold. His private memory of one night in the house had given him a claim, but he feared that speaking it would make the room keep him. He asked Ivara to listen to the spool for him. She refused gently. “It belongs in your hearing.”

He placed the thread against his ear and stepped over the threshold. The room took a breath. The door behind him did not close.

When Nima returned the washer necklace to the Orchard, she did not put it back around her throat. She tied it to the hinge in the empty tray. The metal pieces hung there like a small, uneven curtain. Each washer had once marked a year of her own life; now it marked a year in which the Orchard had cared for something without claiming it.

The other keepers asked what she meant by saying her grandmother had not made the Latch. Nima repeated the four-child story and showed them the strip. Some were relieved. They had never liked the tale of one grandmother building an impossible room alone. Others worried that if nobody owned the Latch, no one would be responsible when it moved.

Nima told them responsibility could be shared. She offered to keep the empty tray open and to report whenever the hinge sang. She would no longer say that the room belonged to her family. The Orchard accepted this modestly: one tree released a fruit, and another turned a leaf toward the stair.

Meanwhile, Ivara followed the thread through the wall and noticed that it did not pass through solid matter. It crossed the wall at a place where a person had once paused. The path was made from attention, not distance. Eren Vey understood this too. He let the spool trail behind him but did not wind it around his wrist. The line stretched only as far as he could continue listening.

Pell recognized a mark on the landing and told them it was one he had scratched as a child. He had made it to remember which direction led back to the first house. In the divided city, the same mark had been copied onto several doorways, and none of them led back. He rubbed it with his thumb. Beneath the old mark, the shell showed a new one: a loop with four smaller loops inside.

The stair was not restoring the way it had been. It was making a way that included their separate memories. The climb became easier once they stopped trying to match their steps. Tovan walked slowly. Nima went ahead. Eren listened at each landing. Ivara and Pell took turns holding the thread.

At the last threshold, they could all hear the same voice, though each of them would later describe it differently. The room had not asked them to agree. It had asked them to arrive.
