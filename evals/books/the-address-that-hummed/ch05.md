# Chapter 5: The Room With Four Names

The room beyond the Nacre Stair had a floor of soft gray felt. It was larger inside than the city could contain. A table stood in the middle, set for five. There were four chairs and one stool, each with a name marked beneath it. Ivara found ALIS on the chair by the wall. On the stool was her own name, written twice: once in her mother's hand, once in a hand she did not recognize.

Alis Noll sat at the table. She had not aged the way people in Oru aged. Her hair was short and white at the temples; her face held the tiredness of an adult who had once waited a very long time as a child. Eren Vey set the brass map before her, though its lines had become threads.

Ivara stopped a pace away. “I thought you were dead.”

“I was dead in the register,” said Alis. “That is not the same thing.”

The truth was plain, but it did not make the years less strange. Alis had stayed in the first house while the rooms divided around her. She could leave only when someone remembered the first house as one place. Those outside could hear her, but they could not agree on where she was. The Hall called its answering chamber the Mouth Room. The Orchard called the missing room the Latch. The Nacre Stair carried its count. These were connected rooms and ways of hearing the first house, not the same room.

Ivara put the glass thimble on the table. “This came from the box.”

Alis took the black thread from inside the thimble, unlooped it from the spool, and looped it around one finger. “Our mother hid a line here so the room would never forget the way back.”

Tovan entered last. He held the bone comb close to his chest. “I kept you safe.”

“You kept me still,” Alis replied. “You knew I was alive and let Ivara mourn me.”

“I thought the city would erase you if it knew.”

“Maybe. But you chose for everyone.”

Tovan's face seemed smaller in the large room. He set the comb on the table. The numbered teeth trembled. Eren Vey produced the spool from his coat. The seventh tooth came loose from its thread and fell beside the comb. Its thread had formed a small loop that fit around the comb's empty place.

The Hall's old examiner had spent years carrying the bone comb, pretending it belonged to a museum. He had been right to fear its power, but wrong to think that withholding it could preserve the first house. It had only made the fragments harder to find.

Nima laid down the bark strip with the four children's names. Beside each was a blank space. “The Orchard can remember the room if it has a keeper,” she said. “The Hall can remember it if it has an address. The stair can remember it if it has a count. None of us can keep it alone.”

Pell sat on the floor. “The Latch never needed a keeper. It needed people to stop calling it theirs.”

At that, the red key, still in Nima's hand, stayed cool. Pell's words were true.

Alis offered Eren Vey a place. He did not take it. “I was there once,” he said. “I don't know what else I can promise.”

“Nothing,” she said. “You can remember one night.”

He told them how his mother carried him through the first house, how every door opened before she touched it, and how the walls gave a low note when she sang. He had kept the memory private, thinking it would make the room claim him. Instead, the lines on the brass map joined into a single loop. Ivara folded the map and kept it.

A door opened in the wall. Beyond it was the Sump, with its clay floor and stone basin. The Understair lay beyond the Sump. The room could now reach the Hall, the Orchard, and the stair. It had become a place again, though not one with a single address.

Ivara asked Alis why she had not called sooner.

“I did. You were too young to know the sound was a name.”

The explanation landed gently. Ivara remembered a lullaby from childhood, a note that made her mother stop sewing and press her hand against the wall. That was when the red key first appeared in her mother's apron. Ivara had carried the key ever since, believing it to be an unclaimed tool. She had not known that it was her mother's promise to come back.

Tovan told Alis he was sorry. She did not forgive him, and she did not send him away. She asked him to carry the comb to the Mouth Room and let it hear the true name of the chamber. He accepted the work without asking for absolution.

Pell gave the tin box to Alis. She turned it over and found a mark on the underside: four small loops and one larger one. The box had been made to store the first house's five names, not its walls. The false bottom had carried the map, thread, thimble, and message; its emptiness had kept a space for the key.

Outside the wall, the Nacre Stair counted 1, 2, 3. It had stopped counting down. The old count had never measured steps. It measured how many names remained before the room was divided beyond repair.

Alis rose from the table. “Let's go tell the Hall it has a neighbor.”

Alis showed them how the first house had survived. When the Hall and Orchard were split, the room's walls did not break. The door that had scarred Eren Vey had not belonged to a vanished wall, as he had believed; it was one of the first house's thresholds, folded away when the room divided. Instead, each fragment received one of its names. The Latch was the name Nima heard at the Orchard. First House was what Mara called it. The child-sized chamber beneath the Understair was its memory of being small. The Mouth Room remained a separate answering chamber, connected to the first house by a shared wall. The fragments remembered the first house, but were not all the same room.

Mara had copied the ancient room plan onto the brass map from memory. She had made it small enough to fold into the box, thinking that a map could preserve a way home. But the map showed the names, not the walls, because walls could move. The black thread was a route held by a person rather than a line. Every time someone remembered a way into the first house, the thread gained another strand.

Alis had never heard the full story about her own arrival. Mara brought her through the Mouth Room as a baby, after finding her alone in a room that had lost its address. The Hall treated her as an unplaced child and prepared to assign her a place in the registry. Mara refused. She said the child had already been found by a room that could hold her. The first house called her Alis Noll and accepted the name.

“I don't know who gave me the name before that,” Alis said. “Maybe no one. Maybe the room did.”

The account changed the way Ivara understood her sister. Alis had not been taken from her family by the Partition. She had been brought into it, and then the Partition hid the room that had welcomed her. The Hall's record was accurate about Alis being unplaced at birth, but wrong to treat that as a defect to be corrected.

Tovan listened without interruption. When Alis finished, he confessed that he had written “buried in the niche” on the old ledger because he could not bear to leave the line blank. He thought a completed form would protect Ivara from a question she was too young to ask. Instead, his neat writing gave her a false memory.

Alis let him finish. “You may carry the comb,” she said. “You may not decide what it does.”

He nodded. That was not forgiveness, but it was a boundary he understood.

Pell found a drawer beneath the table. Inside was a paper bird folded from a strip of an old address. He knew the handwriting: his own, from when he was a child. On its wing he had written “Pell Aven.” He gave the bird to Eren Vell, who read the name and then tucked it into his coat. For years, Pell had believed the porter name was the only one the Hall would let him keep. Now he had the old name as well, not as a replacement but as a piece of his history.

Nima asked whether the Latch would return to the Orchard. Alis said it could visit, but it would not be stored inside a tree again. A room that belonged to several people could not be put away in one person's container. Nima agreed. She would leave the empty tray open, with the hinge visible, so visitors could see where the joining had been.

The table shook once. The four chairs and the stool shifted to face the open door. The first house had finished remembering its way back.

The room kept its own time. They ate nothing, slept not at all, yet each felt the afternoon pass as surely as if a bell had marked it. Alis said the first house did not measure hours. It measured visits. Whenever someone came in, the room made room for that person's memory. When someone left, it kept the shape of the memory without keeping the person.

Ivara asked whether Alis had been lonely. Alis said yes, and no. For a long time, she had been the only person who could enter. Then, whenever the Hall asked a question through the Mouth Room, she could hear it. Whenever Nima passed the empty tray, Alis could hear the washer. She had been surrounded by people who did not know how close they were.

“That's a peculiar kind of company,” Ivara said.

“It is. I don't recommend it.”

They laughed. The room responded by shifting the table a little closer to the door. The movement felt less like magic than courtesy.

Alis showed Ivara a mark under the stool, made by a child with a blunt tool. It was a crooked line that looked like a road. Ivara remembered drawing it in the bark dust while Mara read aloud. She had no memory of the room itself, only the warmth beneath the drawer where she slept. The mark gave the warmth a shape.

Pell told them what he remembered of the four children: Nima had insisted every room needed a name; Alis wanted no name that could be used as a lock; he had argued for a door that could be folded away; and Ivara, too small to join the argument, had chosen the place where all their voices could be heard. The story made Ivara feel as though she had helped build the house, even if she could not remember doing it.

Tovan asked Alis whether the room would hold him now. She said it already did, but that was not the same as excusing what he had done. He accepted the distinction. He sat at the fifth place and listened while the others spoke about the years between then and now. No one made him the center of the story, but no one pushed him outside it.

By the time the map's lines had settled, they could see the route each person had taken to reach the table. The routes were different. All of them led to a seat.
