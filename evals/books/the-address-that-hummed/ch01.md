# Chapter 1: The Address That Hummed

In the city of Oru, no one gave directions by pointing. A finger could indicate a wall, but a wall might be elsewhere by supper. People navigated by listening for the rooms they wanted. A kitchen with a remembered argument clicked its pipes. A nursery hummed a lullaby even after its children had grown old. The Registry called these sounds addresses. Everyone else called them manners.

Ivara Noll worked in the Vellum Hall, where addresses were copied onto strips of skin-thin bark and pinned to a map that had no north. Her desk stood beside the Mouth Room, a chamber inside the Hall where new rooms were asked to say what they were. Its door was a circular slab of black wood with a brass lip. When it opened, the room inside gave a note like a spoon touching a cup.

“Not a room,” said Tovan Sere, listening with his left ear. He was the Hall's senior examiner, though the younger clerks called him the Chair because he never stood while deciding anything. “A pocket. A room has a door that expects to be used.”

Ivara wrote pocket on the Mouth Room's bark strip. The word made the Mouth Room's note flatten. She scratched it out.

At her elbow sat a red key, a long iron thing with a forked tooth. Her mother had left it with no label and no lock to fit. Ivara kept it because it warmed when she lied. She had tested this carefully, first telling it that she liked turnip broth, then that she had never entered the Understair. It warmed at the second claim. The Understair was a maintenance passage below Vellum Hall, officially sealed since Ivara was small.

Across the aisle, Eren Vey sorted address strips by the pressure of their sound. He was a careful young clerk whose voice went thin at the end of every sentence, as if the rest of him had stepped through a door. Three desks away, Eren Vell, a porter with the same narrow face and almost the same name, carried stacks of bark to the drying racks. The two Eren’s had learned to answer only to their surnames. When someone called “Eren,” both looked up and Tovan deducted a minute of their pay.

The city had sent a petition that morning: a room was missing from the Tin Orchard. The Orchard was not a garden, though it contained rust-colored trees. Each tree grew a hollow metal fruit in which a family could keep a private address. Somewhere among the trees was a little room called the Latch, where the Orchard's caretaker stored spare door hinges. The petition said the Latch had vanished without taking its hinge inventory with it. That was impossible. Rooms did not leave their contents behind.

“Who signed?” asked Tovan.

“A name, Lio,” said Ivara. “I took it for a woman’s name. It says she is the last person who remembers the door.”

“Then we have two problems,” said the Chair. “The room and the memory.”

Tovan sent Ivara and Eren Vey to inspect the Orchard. Before she left, Ivara slid the red key into her cuff. Eren Vell stopped at the Hall's outer door to hand her a parcel tied with blue thread. “For the Orchard keeper,” he said. “A tin box from the lost-property shelf. Don't open it. It has been waiting longer than I have.”

The tin box was warm, too. Ivara put the tin box in her satchel and kept the key in her cuff. She did not ask why the parcel had no label. In the Registry, a question without an address could wander for days.

The Tin Orchard rang in the afternoon, each metal fruit making a different note as the branches shifted. The trees grew in shallow trays of pale grit. Their roots were loops of wire, and their leaves were tiny hinged tags that clicked together like teeth. Nima Orrel, the Orchard keeper, came to meet them. She wore a necklace of brass washers, one for each year she had lived in the Orchard.

“Lio isn't a person,” Nima said. “It's what the missing room calls itself when it wants to be found.” Ivara understood that the petition had mistaken a room's name for its signer.

Ivara glanced at Eren. “You signed the petition.”

“I signed the petition that arrived,” said Nima. “The ink was already there.”

She led them to a tray with an empty ring of grit. A single hinge lay in the center. Its pin bore a faded blue stain. Ivara thought of Eren Vell's parcel, still in her bag.

“Who was here last?” asked Eren Vey.

“I was,” Nima said. “I put the hinge down. I heard the Latch ask me to keep quiet. Then the path ended.”

The false certainty in her voice came from practice. Nima believed the Latch had been built by Nima's grandmother, and that her grandmother had hidden it to protect the Orchard. Everyone in Oru knew the tale: the old woman, the hinge, the room that slipped away. Ivara had copied the official version herself.

But there was no sound in the empty ring. A vanished room should leave an echo. Ivara crouched and heard instead a faint tapping from inside the grit. She took out the red key. Its prongs trembled toward the ground.

“That key is not on the inventory,” Nima said.

“Neither is this room,” said Ivara.

She turned the key in the grit. A narrow seam opened, no wider than a thread, and from below came a voice that sounded exactly like the Mouth Room's note. The Orchard had a floor under its floor.

Eren Vey caught her sleeve before she could put her fingers into the seam. “We should record this first.”

“If we record it, the Hall will close it.”

“If we don't, the room can move.”

Ivara looked at the empty hinge ring, the blue thread, and the parcel in her satchel. She knew, then, that Eren Vell had sent her the box for a reason. She did not yet know what he had put inside.

Nima set her palm over the seam. “My grandmother made the Latch,” she said. “It belongs to our family.”

The red key stayed cool in Ivara's hand. Nima believed the inherited story, though the story was wrong.

That night, Ivara did not go home. The Hall's sleeping gallery held thirty narrow bunks, each with a tag that named its occupant and one room they were permitted to dream in. Her tag said “Ivara Noll: Vellum Hall.” She turned it over. On the back, someone had written Alis Noll in faded red ink.

The letters reminded Ivara of her mother's old recipe sheets, but the hand was not Mara's. Mara Noll wrote every letter with a small gap in its middle, as though each word had a door in it. Ivara went to her desk and searched the drawers for the sheet where Mara had recorded a recipe for shell milk. She did not find it. The name on the back of her sleeping tag had not come from this page. The letters had faded, but the writing itself was recent; someone had added the name since Mara's last recipe sheet.

She found Eren Vey still at his desk. “Did you put this on my tag?”

He inspected it without touching. “No. The tag is telling you where it thinks you belong.”

“Tags don't think.”

“They don't have to. They only need a little pressure from the room.”

He explained that the first Hall clerks had not pinned addresses to a map. They pinned them to people. When a room moved, the people who had used it carried its location in their habits: how they paused at a turn, what they called a threshold, which side of a cup they reached for. Later clerks replaced these living records with tags and maps. Eren believed the Hall still listened to people, even when it pretended to listen only to paper.

“Why did you come here?” Ivara asked.

“My mother said I was born in the wrong room.” He tapped his own tag. It held no birthplace. “I want to know what she meant.”

The question made Ivara think of the report from the Orchard. Nima had signed it without remembering signing. The name Lio had appeared without a hand to write it. Perhaps a room could use a person's habits, too, and borrow a name when it had no other way to knock.

Eren showed her a thin scar across his wrist. A door had closed on it when he was very young, he said, but the door had been in a wall that no longer existed. He had never told the Registry. “If a room can keep part of you, then maybe it can keep a person.”

Ivara touched the red key through her cuff. It was cold. She hadn't lied, but she had kept a thought to herself: she sometimes heard a quiet second note under the Mouth Room's tone. Now it came again, so soft she could have blamed the pipes.

At dawn, the Hall called all clerks to the map. A new strip of bark had appeared by the Orchard's address. It bore no words, only a pair of small handprints. The younger clerks laughed and said a child had touched the wet bark. Tovan said nothing. He turned the strip face down and ordered Ivara to leave it alone.

She turned it face up again once the Chair had gone. The handprints were the exact size of her own hands before she could speak.

At the end of the morning shift, Ivara took the handprint strip to Tovan. He studied it for a long while, then brought out a ledger with a cracked spine. The book recorded the Hall's repairs, with a few private notes Tovan had added about residents. On the first page, in the same red ink, was a list of places that had been joined and separated. Beside each place was the name of the clerk who authorized the work.

Mara Noll appeared beside the first division. Tovan's name appeared beside the second. Ivara asked what the marks meant. He told her that Mara had argued against dividing the first house, but eventually signed the order to protect the children inside. He had signed the follow-up that made the division permanent.

“Did she tell you what happened to Alis?”

“She asked me to keep the address quiet.”

“That's not the same thing.”

“No,” Tovan said. “It isn't.”

He shut the ledger before she could read the other names. Ivara noticed a mark beside Eren Vey's birthplace, an open door drawn in blue. Tovan saw her notice it and did not cover the mark. That, more than his answer, told her that he had begun to understand what he owed them.

She left the Hall with the handprint strip tucked into her coat. Outside, the city had no streets in the usual sense. There were lanes made by repeated journeys, and the lanes grew firmer whenever enough people traveled them for the same reason. The path to the Orchard had become a shallow groove in the stone. Ivara followed it, listening to its small echo beneath her shoes.

At the gate, Nima's necklace made a distant chime. The Orchard keeper had begun the evening count, one washer for each tree that had offered its fruit to a family. The necklace counted Nima's years; this was a separate count. Ivara understood now why the trees were called an orchard even though they grew no food. Each one held a memory with a hard shell around it. The fruit could be opened only by the person who had put the address inside.

Somewhere under the grit, the missing room tapped once. Ivara pressed the handprint strip to the ground. For a breath, she felt a child's fingers push back from the other side.
