# Chapter 6: A Neighboring Address

They returned by the Nacre Stair, which now climbed without counting. The Tin Orchard opened its gate as they approached. The metal leaves stopped clicking and held still, listening. Nima went ahead to tell the trees that the Latch had never belonged to her grandmother alone. She spoke the corrected story without apology. Some of the trees rang in agreement; others stayed quiet. An Orchard could be stubborn without being cruel.

At the Vellum Hall, Tovan carried the bone comb into the Mouth Room. He asked it to say the true name. The black circular door opened, and the chamber inside sounded not like a spoon touching a cup, but like five spoons laid carefully on a table.

“First House,” Ivara said.

The room answered by opening a second door. Through it, they saw Alis standing in the Latch, with the tin box on the stool Alis had used in the first house. Nima still held the red key. From that day, the Hall and the first house shared a wall. Clerks could visit the room, but they had to ask before recording anything inside it.

The decision upset the Registry. A room without a single registered address was, to the clerks, an argument rather than a place. Tovan stood before the Hall's assembly and admitted that he had known Alis was alive. He also admitted that he had hidden the seventh tooth, and that his claim that the Latch could not open again was false. He did not claim that fear excused him. He asked to remain examiner until someone else could hear rooms as well as he could.

The assembly gave him one day a week, and assigned the other six to Eren Vey. The two Eren’s signed the same sheet. Eren Vell kept the name he had chosen after the Hall erased Pell Aven. On the reverse side of the form, he wrote “Pell Aven” so the old name would not vanish. Both names were his; neither was the Hall's property.

Ivara asked Alis what the red key opened. Alis placed it on the table.

“Nothing,” Alis said. “It reminds a door that it can choose.”

Nima handed the key to Alis. It had been Mara's, then Ivara's, and now it belonged to the person who had waited longest for it. Alis placed it in the tin box, whose false bottom folded down to make an ordinary bottom at last. The brass map stayed with Ivara, the glass thimble returned to the Mouth Room, and the numbered spool went to Eren Vey. The bone comb remained with Tovan. Each object had a new place, though no ledger agreed about which place mattered most.

The chalk moth stayed in the hidden chamber beneath the Understair. Once a month, Pell visited it and told it one thing that had changed. He never tried to remove it. The moth had become the chamber's small guardian, flat enough to fit in a book and stubborn enough to hold a name.

Nima continued keeping the Tin Orchard, but she stopped wearing one washer for each year. She untied the washers from the hinge and threaded them onto a cord around a young tree instead. If someone asked whether her grandmother had made the Latch, Nima said, “She helped remember it.” The answer did not satisfy everyone. It was accurate.

Alis came to the Hall on the first day of every new cycle. She sat in the Mouth Room and let the clerks ask their questions. Sometimes she answered. Sometimes the chamber answered first. Neither one was mistaken for the other anymore.

Ivara kept her desk beside the shared wall. A new strip of bark lay there, blank except for a line that read: “A room may have more than one address.” She had not written it. The bark had grown that sentence from a pressure in the wall.

One evening, while the Hall was closing, Eren Vey heard a soft note come from beneath Ivara's desk. He placed the numbered spool on the floor and let it turn. It rolled toward the wall and stopped at a crack no wider than a hair.

The first house had begun to hum.

Ivara did not take out the key. She did not call the examiner. She knelt and listened. On the other side, a child laughed, then someone older laughed with them. The sounds belonged to different people, though they shared a room.

The Vellum Hall's map acquired a second loop. No clerk tried to cut it away. By morning, the map had moved the Hall's desk, the Orchard's hinge, and the stair's first step a little closer together. The city of Oru remained where it had always been, if “where” could still be said of a place that listened.

The blank address strip filled itself with five names: Alis Noll, Ivara Noll, Pell Aven, Nima Orrel, and Eren Vey. Beneath them, in a smaller hand, appeared Eren Vell. The porter looked at the strip and smiled at the clerk. “Both,” he said, when Ivara asked which name should go on the file.

She left the strip unpinned after that. Some things were easier to find when no one tried to own their names.

There were practical matters after the ceremony. The Hall's clerks needed a way to write an address for a room that had several doors and no fixed boundary. Ivara proposed that they list the names people used, then leave a space for any name the room might choose later. The senior clerks objected that an empty line could not be audited. Eren Vey pointed out that the old records had contained full names and still lost an entire person.

Tovan sat beside the map and said nothing. When asked, he offered no defense of the old system. He said he could teach the younger clerks to listen before they wrote. His examiner's chair was moved beside the shared wall, so that he could hear both the Hall and the first house. The Chair no longer had to remain seated to make a decision; he chose the new place himself.

Nima returned to the Orchard for a single afternoon. She left the empty tray open and told the trees that the red key had passed through their hands. The trees kept the impression of the iron in the grit. That was enough.

Pell visited the Hall's record room and asked for the winter roll where Pell Aven had disappeared. The clerk who brought it out could not find a line for him. Pell wrote his own name in the margin and signed Eren Vell beneath it. The two names sat next to each other without an arrow between them. A new clerk asked whether that meant they were two people. Pell answered, “It means I am here twice in the book.”

The clerk left the entry as it was.

Eren Vey continued to hear the spool's quiet song. He used it to find rooms that had been pushed behind walls or folded into stairs, but he never pulled the thread without asking who else could hear it. Sometimes it led to an empty cupboard. Sometimes it led to a room whose occupant had forgotten they were allowed to open the door. He brought no rooms to the Hall by force.

Ivara found a drawer in her desk that had not been there before. It contained the shell-milk recipe sheet she had searched for, in Mara Noll's handwriting. The recipe was for shell milk, but beneath the ingredients Mara had written a note on the recipe sheet: “If Alis comes home by a different door, let her choose which one is hers.” Ivara kept the sheet beside the blank address strip. Her mother had known the return might happen, though she had not known when or how.

When Ivara asked Alis what she wanted the Hall to call her, Alis answered “Alis” and nothing more. The register printed “Alis Noll” because that was the name the room had kept for her. No one wrote “unplaced.”

On the next cycle's first day, the five names on the strip rearranged themselves. The order changed, but each remained legible. The clerk responsible for the map tried to pin the strip down. The pin bent. Ivara told him to let it move. He looked uncomfortable, then tucked the pin back into his sleeve.

That evening, the Hall doors stayed open late. People came from the Orchard and the lower stair to hear the shared wall hum. Some brought old names; others brought no names at all. The room did not answer everyone. It did not have to. Being heard was not the price of being allowed to enter.

When the lamps dimmed, Ivara went to the Mouth Room. She asked if it wanted an official address. The chamber answered with five soft notes, then a pause for a sixth. Ivara waited. A sixth note came from the other side of the wall, not from the room but from a person walking home.

The Hall altered its forms slowly. First it added a box for “other names.” Then it added a line for “room's chosen address.” The oldest clerks insisted that these were temporary measures. The youngest clerks filled the boxes with names they had never been allowed to record before. One wrote down the name a stair used for itself. Another entered a room that was known only by the sound of someone laughing in it.

Ivara reviewed the changes, not as a new examiner but as a clerk who knew what it felt like to see a familiar name on an unfamiliar line. She returned the forms that treated a person as a room's possession. She accepted the forms that recorded who had chosen an address and who had chosen to leave it blank.

Tovan trained himself to listen with both ears. The comb stayed on his desk, but he stopped using it to divide a room's notes. He taught that a sound could have several sources and still be one sound. Some trainees found this difficult. He let them struggle, as he had once refused to let others do.

Nima came to the Hall with a small metal fruit from the Orchard. It contained no address. She opened it anyway. Inside was only a pale ring of grit, the outline left by the Latch's old hinge. She set the fruit beside the map. Ivara wrote no label on it. They let it sit without deciding what it meant.

Eren Vell kept the paper bird in his coat. The porter still carried bark strips from one desk to another, but now he could choose which name to give when someone asked who he was. Sometimes he said Eren Vell. Sometimes he wrote Pell Aven. When someone called Eren, both men still looked up, and the younger clerks still laughed. The joke no longer cost anyone a minute of pay.

Alis never moved into a single room. She lived where she chose from day to day, sometimes in the Hall, sometimes in the first house, and sometimes among the trees. No ledger followed her unless she invited it. She was not a mystery the city had solved. She was a person whose life had become visible again.

The last sound Ivara heard before the Hall closed was the shared wall giving its six notes. She could name five of them. The sixth belonged to whoever was coming home. The Hall did not ask for that person's name before opening the door.
