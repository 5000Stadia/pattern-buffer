# Chapter 2: A Name for the Wind

By noon, the posted notice had curled inward, though the air in the Silt Choir was still. Or took it down and found the wall behind it wet in the shape of a hand.

Nemi Tallow had not returned to her stall. Or found her instead in the narrow lane that ran behind the market, standing beneath a drainpipe with her apron turned inside out. The blue tooth rested in a fold of cloth. It made a tiny clicking noise whenever Or looked away.

“You said it would call what it remembers,” Or said.

“It has begun.” Nemi tucked the cloth into a tin case, then pushed the case under the loose hem of her skirt. “I heard the orchard bells in it. So did Cale.”

“Does it belong to the orchard?”

“It belonged to a tree before there was an orchard.”

Or waited. Nemi’s silences were usually for sale, but this one seemed to have a price she did not know how to name.

“Tell me what you know.”

“I know a person can carry a weather-memory without knowing it. I know the tooth is a key. I know that if the old cisterns hear it, they will open.”

“What is in them?”

“Rain, if we are lucky.”

“And if we aren’t?”

“Something that thinks rain is a person.”

They heard footsteps. Nemi drew Or into the drain’s shadow as Vael Senn came through the lane. Vael, the senior weather clerk, was Oruva’s aunt by the town’s loose use of kinship, though no blood joined them. She wore a white collar with three black stitches, the mark of a person allowed to certify weather. Vael had the same narrow chin as Or and none of her patience.

“Or,” Vael said. “You are to come with me.”

“Why?”

“Because you were in the Room Below Mouth this morning.”

“I’m often in the Room Below Mouth.”

“This morning the first jar began to sing.” Vael’s eyes went briefly to Nemi’s skirt. “There is a record of every sound in that House.”

“Then you know it was not me.”

“I know you were there when it happened.”

Nemi stepped out from the shadow. “If you have a question, ask it to both of us.”

Vael regarded her. “Nemi Tallow, your license to trade containers was suspended three years ago.”

“It was restored.”

“By whom?”

“By someone who understood what an empty thing is for.”

Vael’s expression did not change. “Come to the Counting Room at Low Weather. Both of you.”

Low Weather was a district built at the bottom of a bowl in the town’s eastern rim. The houses there had low doors and ceilings, so that even tall visitors felt the sky pressing down. The Counting Room sat below a stairwell, at the end of a corridor that smelled of chalk. Its windows faced inward, toward a courtyard roofed with green glass.

In the room, a long table held ledgers, scales and one blue-black bowl. Iven Orri sat at the far end, the town’s appointed Listener. Iven was a slight, old person with a shaved head and a voice like dry paper. The title Listener belonged to anyone who could hear unspent weather in a sealed vessel. There were two Listeners in Ors; Iven was one, and the other had not been seen for years.

“Which jar?” Iven asked.

Vael set a ledger before him. “First North Drizzle, Year Forty-One.”

“That jar is empty,” said Vael. “It has always been empty.” Or recognized it as the cracked First North Drizzle jar, though the nameless jar beside the ledgers was a different vessel. Vael meant that it held nothing now; she believed it had never held rain.

Or looked from the ledger to the bowl. “I heard it.”

“Then you heard something else,” Vael said.

Iven leaned forward, his pale eyes fixed on Or. “What did it sound like?”

“Like rain on the inside of clay.”

He shut his eyes. “I hear nothing.”

“You don’t have to hear it to know it happened,” Vael said. “The seal was intact. No water was found.”

“Was the seal blue?” Nemi asked.

Vael turned sharply. “Why would you ask that?”

Nemi smiled without warmth. “Because I trade in containers.”

Or could tell Nemi had hidden the tooth in a fold of cloth, but could not see that Nemi had tucked that cloth into a tin case beneath her skirt. She did not know that Nemi had moved the case to her left pocket, or that Nemi had just decided to give it to Or before sunset. Iven, meanwhile, understood that the jar had spoken, but could not hear the voice clearly enough to tell anyone what it said.

Vael closed the ledger. “The Bell Orchard will be inspected at dusk. Cale Rusk claims the tree roots are drinking from the cistern. If the orchard is responsible for the missing rain, we will burn it.”

“Burn it with what?” Nemi asked. “Your empty jars?”

“Do not mistake a lack of water for a lack of authority.”

Or placed her brass tide spoon on the table. “If there is rain in the orchard, the jar may be remembering it.”

Vael examined the spoon. “This is not evidence.”

“It knows when a jar is lying.”

“That is superstition.”

Iven opened his eyes. “Superstition is a word for evidence that has not learned to sit still.”

Vael’s hand hovered over the spoon, then withdrew. “You are both dismissed. Or, return the spoon to the House. Nemi, surrender your containers for inspection.”

At the doorway, Nemi brushed her skirt against Or’s knuckles. The tin case slipped into Or’s coat pocket. She did not look down.

“Dusk,” Nemi murmured. “Come to the orchard. Bring nothing that rings.”

Outside, the town’s empty clouds had gathered above Low Weather, pressed together like folded cloth. Or touched the case through her coat. It clicked once, softly, in time with the distant bells.

She knew Vael believed the orchard had stolen the rain. She knew Cale had said the tooth must be returned. She did not yet know that the two beliefs could both be wrong, or that one of them had been placed in her mind deliberately.

Or spent the afternoon on the roof of the Counting Room, where clerks tested the air for signs of weather. The roof was paved with thin tiles that turned color if a cloud passed overhead. They had been gray for decades. Today, one tile near the eastern edge showed a damp green crescent.

The roof tender was called Pell, though the office register gave him the name Pell of the Fourth Stair. He kept a tray of dry moss and a pair of little brushes. When Or pointed out the green tile, he examined it without touching.

“Was it green yesterday?” she asked.

“It was gray.”

“Does the tile only change when it rains?”

“It changes when the roof believes it has rained.”

“That’s not very useful.”

“Useful things are often less exact than useless ones.” Pell brushed the moss from the seam. “The roof has a long memory. It remembers the last time the orchard bells were all ringing. It may be confusing the present with that.”

He showed Or the roof’s old color chart. The green crescent meant “rain approaching from below.” There were no other marks in the chart for rain approaching from below. Or copied the phrase into her notebook, and Pell told her not to write it in the official ledger unless she was prepared to explain it to Vael.

On the way down, Or stopped in the House’s archive alcove. The archive held the town’s oldest weather calendars: narrow strips of bark tied into bundles. Each strip listed the time, quantity, and direction of every rain since Ors began storing it. The entries were precise. A rain from the north could be distinguished from a mist from the east by the shape of a clerk’s punctuation. She found the year forty-one and checked the first north drizzle. It was marked as “not received.” A note in the margin said: “No north wind, no drizzle.”

The handwriting resembled Vael’s, though the ink was too old to belong to her. Under the note, almost erased, was a second sentence: “Seal anyway.”

Or copied that too. She folded the strip back into its bundle and tied it with the same thread. Nothing was missing from the archive. Nothing had been taken. She had only discovered that a record could be made to say two different things, depending on which line a person read first.

She brought the new information to Tavin, who was taking inventory of empty jars in the public hall. “Do the old calendars ever contradict themselves?”

“Only when two clerks disagree.”

“What if they both wrote the truth?”

“Then they disagree more quietly.”

He glanced at her notebook. “You’re writing too much.”

“I’m writing exactly as much as I need.”

“Those are not the same amount.”

He showed her the public jars, each one marked for a household. The town divided its rain by need, but the names on the jars were not family names. A baker’s jar was called First Crust. A child’s vessel was called Sleep. The weather clerks believed a name helped the water remember its purpose. Or wondered whether the old jar had been named to help the rain remember something else.

As she left, Tavin gave her a small clay cup. “This one has no allotment name. It was left in the hall.”

Or took it. The cup was dry and ordinary, but at the bottom there was a blue smear. She wrapped it in her scarf and carried it back toward the House. She did not know whether the blue came from wax, from rain, or from the tooth. She only knew the town had begun leaving evidence in places where no one expected to find it.

That evening Or went to the eastern steps to watch the empty clouds. In Ors, children learned to identify weather by its absence. A blank cloud was not the same as a clear sky: a clear sky had a grain to it, a faint brightness that made the roofs look newly built. The clouds now looked like folded paper, with no brightness between them.

A group of children had gathered below the stairs, comparing the names their families gave to rain. One child called it “roof milk”; another called it “the hush.” The smallest said her grandmother called it “come home.” Or listened without correcting them. The town had long treated weather names as labels on jars. The children treated them as things one might say to another person.

When she came back to the House, she found a blue smear on the tin case’s hinge. She put it beside the unallotted clay cup Tavin had given her. The cup’s glaze reflected the smear, though the two objects did not touch. Or moved the case across the table. The reflection followed it.

She took a paper label from the archive and wrote “empty” on it, then stuck it to the cup. The blue reflection disappeared. She removed the label, and it returned. She tried another word, “rain,” and the reflection sharpened into a tiny crescent. The cup had some relationship to the tooth, but Or could not tell whether the word called it or merely gave her a way to notice it.

She wrote down the test without including the conclusion. She had learned from the old calendars that a record could be made to sound certain after the evidence had grown faint. She would rather leave a question visible than turn it into an instruction.

Before sleeping, she took the public cup to the window. Nothing came through the dry air. She set it on the sill anyway. In the morning, the cup was wet inside, and the roof outside remained dry.

She returned the public cup to Tavin at the end of his shift. He asked whether it had held anything. Or said it had held a little water. Tavin checked its base and found no leak. He did not ask where the water came from. Instead, he wrote “wet” beside the cup’s inventory number, then paused and added “once.” That was the first time Or saw him record something without pretending it would remain true forever.

Or asked Tavin if he knew why Vael had ordered the orchard burned. He said it was not an order, only a warning. Or pointed out that the distinction would not comfort the orchard. Tavin admitted that the clerks had begun to speak in warnings because they had no authority to repair the channel. He had thought the rules kept people safe. Now he wondered whether rules could be used to keep people from noticing danger. Or told him he could help by remembering what he saw, even if he could not change it.

By night, the clouds had pressed lower over Low Weather. Or watched them from the stairwell, where the rooftops seemed close enough to touch. The green tile on the Counting Room had gone gray again. The change made no sense as a forecast, but it matched the pattern of the blue reflection in the cup: something had appeared, answered a question, then gone quiet. Or decided to keep both observations, even if they did not yet fit together.

Or tucked Tavin’s note into the archive. The word “once” remained visible beside the cup’s number.
