# Chapter 8: The Ledger of Rain

Vael took the map to the Counting Room. Or followed with Iven, carrying the jar wrapped in the flour sack. Before they reached Low Weather, the weather clerks had assembled at the Silt Choir. They stood beneath the market awning with empty measuring cups in their hands.

Vael climbed onto a bench. “The first drop was a trick of the lanterns,” she announced, repeating the claim from her earlier notice. “There was no drop from the sky. The orchard remains dry. The cistern remains sealed.”

A man in the crowd lifted his cup. “I caught one.”

“Then you caught dew.”

“It was cold.”

“Dew is cold.”

Or stood at the back, where she could see the wall behind the notice. The hand-shaped stain had spread down to the cobbles. A narrow rivulet ran from it, against the slope, and vanished into the drain.

Iven went to Vael. “You know it rained.”

“I know what people need to believe.”

“That is not the same as knowing what happened.”

“You should understand. A town is a vessel. If you crack it, it leaks.”

“Perhaps it was already leaking.”

Vael folded the map and slipped it into the inside pocket of her collar. Or saw her do it. Later, she would realize Vael had not hidden the map from the people; she had hidden it from the other clerks.

At the Counting Room, Vael opened the old ledgers. The first ledger had pages made from pressed kelp. It smelled of salt. The second ledger was written on thin, translucent horn. The third contained only a list of names, no dates.

“Three Listeners,” Iven said. “The first, Vey Orin. The second, Mara Senn. The third—”

“Is not in this book,” Vael said.

Or read the names. “What if the third was never a person?”

“Then why would the storm say not to count me twice?”

“Maybe the storm said not to count the wrong person.”

Iven put his hand on the horn pages. “There’s a name beneath the ink.”

Vael scraped gently with a bone knife. The letters emerged in pale lines: Oruva Senn.

Or’s name.

She felt the room tighten. “I was born after Mara was hidden.”

“Your birth record is not in the town’s weather ledger,” Vael said. “It is in the first jar.”

“Why would a jar hold my birth?”

“The storm was being named as you were born. The clerks wrote the third name in the weather record so the town would forget it.”

Or shook her head. “I’m not a Listener.”

“You are. You hear the sealed weather. You carried the spoon. You found the tooth.”

“I did those things because I was curious.”

“You did them because the storm knew your name.”

Iven looked at her. “And because you heard it answer.”

Or removed the brass tide spoon from her sleeve and placed it on the ledger. “If I’m the third Listener, why didn’t you tell me?”

Vael lowered her voice. “Because your mother asked me not to.”

“Is she alive?”

“I know she lived. I don’t know where she is now.”

“You said she died.”

“I said I believed she died. The record was a lie, but I did not write it.”

Or remembered the hooded visitor who had carried jars into the Room Below Mouth. That person had always left before the lamps were lit. Once, the visitor had touched Or’s shoulder, and the hand had smelled of the red dye-house. Her mother used to smell of that dye, too.

“Who brought the jars?” Or asked.

Vael looked at the floor. “A clerk called Mara.”

“You knew?”

“I did not know it was your mother until yesterday.”

“Then who was she?”

“Your mother.”

Vael opened the map. On the reverse, in a different hand, was written: Vey Orin is the woman with the copper hand. She is not the storm. She is its first witness.

Or touched the writing. The woman on the causeway, the stranger in the orchard, and the missing first Listener were the same person. The name Vey had not been a convenient invention. It was a piece of her own life that the town had cut away.

“Where is she now?” Or asked.

“The orchard.”

“Then we go there.”

Vael shook her head. “The cistern will open only when all three names are spoken together.”

“Then we find the third.”

“You are the third.”

“I’m not speaking a name I don’t understand.”

Iven took up the brass spoon and returned it to Or. “You already understand the first part. A name is a door. The person decides whether to open it.”

Before dusk, the clerks locked the Counting Room. Vael kept the map. Iven lifted the blue tooth from the blue bowl and held it. Or carried the cracked jar, the rain vial, and the small black seed. She left the old ledger open on the table. Its pages fluttered once though the windows were shut.

On the final page, new writing had appeared: THE FOURTH NAME IS THE ONE THAT WAS LEFT OUT.

After the gathering at the Silt Choir, Or went to the roof tender’s room to see the marks on the tiles. The office had closed, but the roof access was unlocked. Pell stood beneath the green glass, brushing away dust from the tiles. The green crescent had spread into a thin line that ran westward.

“Does that mean rain?” Or asked.

“It means the roof believes it has a route.”

“Can a roof believe?”

“Not like we do. It remembers what has happened on it.” He pointed to the line. “This is the direction the water used to take.”

Or stood over the glass and saw the market court below. The old market basin was hidden under three layers of stone. Rain had once moved from the orchard through the town, past the dye-house, then out through the west gate. The town had sealed each section as it expanded. The weather clerks had kept measuring rain after the path was blocked, pretending the vessels were a substitute for a route.

Pell found a separate old tile chart inside a cupboard. The chart showed the roof when it was new, with arrows indicating where rain should go. The west edge had a notch like a bite. The mark matched the shape of the blue tooth.

“Did the orchard bells show the way?” Or asked.

“They were one part of it. The roof answered the bells. The market basin answered the roof. The channel carried what remained.”

“So every part was connected.”

“Everything is connected if you can wait long enough.”

Pell gave her the tile chart. It had no official value and no record number. Or rolled Pell’s tile chart and carried it to the Counting Room. She placed it beside the old map. The routes matched. A thin blue line crossed the drawing where the old market basin should be, then continued beyond the edge of town.

Iven studied the chart. “The storm has a way out.”

“Then why did we keep it?”

“Because people thought if it left, it would take the rain with it.”

“Will it?”

“I don’t know.”

“Would you let it go?”

Iven sat down, then stood again. “I was trained to listen. I was not trained to decide.”

They heard a crash from the public hall. A row of jars had fallen from a shelf. Their names had been scratched away. One by one, the jars rolled toward the west wall and stopped in a line, mouths pointing to the market court.

Or set Pell’s tile chart against them. The route on the paper aligned with the jars. None of the clerks had moved them. It seemed the vessels themselves had arranged a map.

Vael came in and picked up one jar. “They are empty.”

The jar’s label was gone, but its glaze held a faint ring of blue. Vael’s certainty had begun to loosen. She no longer insisted there was no rain, but she still treated every unexplained event as if it might be an attack.

“Will you let us open the channel?” Or asked.

“If the map is right.”

“And if it isn’t?”

“Then we will have learned something before the town floods.”

Or understood that Vael had not been trying to destroy the orchard. She had been trying to protect a town whose history she did not trust. That did not make her earlier lie true. It made it harder to dismiss her as a villain.

Iven borrowed the original bargain ledger after the gathering. He carried it to the House and read the bargain in a room where the vessels could hear him. The pages had been copied, revised, and shortened many times. In each version, one clause disappeared. The first said that the storm would pass through and the town would let its name travel with it. A later copy said the town would keep its name. The newest version said only that rain would be stored and measured.

Or asked which version was true. Iven replied that all of them described a decision someone had made. The weather had not changed the words; the town had.

They tried speaking the first version aloud. The vessels on the shelves rattled, but none opened. They tried the second. The blue bowl remained still. When they read the shortened version, the jar at the bottom shelf gave a single tap.

“It recognizes the lie,” Or said.

“Or it recognizes the version it was made to carry,” Iven said.

They stopped reading. The room settled. Or was beginning to understand that the jars were not giving them answers. They were responding to what the people put into them. The town had stored a storm and then expected the vessels to explain what it wanted.

Or brought Pell’s tile chart and laid it next to the open ledger. The chart’s blue line ended at the old market basin; the ledger’s amended words ended at the point where the town chose to keep its name. Both showed where the passage had been interrupted. Vael copied the original clause into the new ledger, leaving the amendment visible beneath it.

She told Or she would make the public correction once they knew what opening the route would do. Or asked if waiting for certainty was another way to avoid the decision. Vael said it might be. She did not pretend otherwise.

Or rolled Pell’s tile chart and tied it with red thread. It was the first chart Vael had allowed her to keep without asking her to bring it back.

When Or left the Counting Room, she carried the blue bowl. It was empty, but the glass lid had a small crack near its rim. She set the lid on the table and handed the bowl to Iven. “It has held too many things,” she said. He asked what it should hold now. “Whatever it is given,” she said. Iven placed it on the windowsill, where the rain could reach it if the clouds ever opened again.

The clerks who had heard the first rain argued over whether to include their own testimony in the ledger. One said their office had caused the trouble and should not record its own account. Vael answered that silence would leave only the old version. They agreed to write their names beside what they had seen. The entries were short and imperfect. A clerk had felt a cold drop on her cheek; another had heard a roof tile ring. The rain became a fact because each person was willing to be named as a witness.

The blue bowl remained on the windowsill overnight. Condensation touched its rim but did not collect inside. A thin stream ran over the lip and down the outer wall, following a groove that led to the courtyard. In the morning, Iven saw the damp stone and said the bowl had chosen a route of its own. Or said the builders had made the groove. Both explanations could be true.

Vael left the old amendment visible. The open ledger showed both the promise and its alteration.

Pell’s tile chart stayed with Or until the group set out. She noticed the paper had been folded along the same line as the old channel. The crease had made the map easier to carry, but it also hid a small mark at the edge. When she unfolded it flat, the mark showed a narrow exit beyond the town wall. The route had never ended at the market basin; it had only begun there.
