# Chapter 3: The Orchard’s Ears

The Bell Orchard was reached by a causeway of pale, porous stone. At high tide the causeway vanished, though Ors had no sea. At low tide it lay naked across a basin of packed salt, and the orchard’s roots showed beneath the island like fingers searching for a wrist.

Or left Low Weather after the lamps were lit. She had the blue tooth in Nemi’s tin case, the brass tide spoon in her sleeve, and no idea whether either object could help. She had obeyed the order not to bring anything that rang. The case clicked once when she reached the basin. She wrapped it in her scarf.

At the causeway’s midpoint, the woman with a copper hand was waiting. Her right hand was not a glove, Or saw; it was a real hand coated in thin copper plates, each plate fitted over a knuckle. A black scarf covered her hair. She stood with one foot on the stone and one in the salt.

“Are you the other Listener?” Or asked.

“I am no one you need to name.”

“Everyone has a name.”

“Not everyone gets to keep it.”

The woman’s voice carried a cadence Or had heard before, but could not place. It was the careful rhythm of someone reading instructions aloud, though there was no paper in her hand.

“Cale is waiting,” she said. “Do not let him take the tooth.”

“Why?”

“He’ll return it to the tree.”

“Isn’t that where it belongs?”

“Not if the tree has already eaten the thing it was meant to open.”

The woman turned toward the salt basin. Or reached for her sleeve, but the copper plates made a whispering sound as the stranger stepped away. At the next breath, she had crossed the basin without leaving a mark.

On the island, Cale Rusk stood beside the oldest bell tree. Its trunk was pale as bone, and its branches curved down to the ground. The broken branch from the market lay beside him. Its bells were dull green and had no clappers.

“Where is the tooth?” Cale asked.

“Why did you say it belonged here?”

“Because it grew here.”

“Before the orchard?”

He looked at her. “Who told you that?”

“Nemi.”

“Nemi sells a story with every empty jar.”

Or kept her palm over the tin case. “You said the old weather took something.”

“I was wrong.”

She had not expected him to answer so quickly. “What did it take?”

“Nothing. We gave something away.” He bent to lift the broken branch. The copper glove squeaked against the bark. “My grandmother, Cale’s grandmother and the orchard’s last keeper, remembered the first storm. She gave the storm’s name to the tree so the town could store rain without being drowned by it. The tooth is part of that name.”

“And the rest?”

“Was swallowed.”

“By the tree?”

“By the cistern beneath it.”

Cale placed the branch at the tree’s roots. For a second, a tremor passed through the bells. A high, thin note climbed into the evening and stopped before it could become a ring.

Or took the blue tooth from the case. The copper glove lifted, but Cale held his hands still.

“Let me see,” he said.

The tooth rolled across Or’s palm toward the tree. She caught it with the tide spoon. The brass crescent shone a pale blue where it touched the tooth.

The old tree’s roots shifted. One of them drew away from the salt and revealed a hollow in the trunk. Inside lay a small glass flask, green and cloudy, sealed with a plug made from black wax. Or could see no liquid in it. The tree’s bark had grown around the flask as if the bottle were a seed.

“That flask is the rain’s first vessel,” Cale said. “My grandmother hid it there when she gave the name.”

“You told Vael the roots were drinking the cistern.”

“I wanted the inspectors to come. Vael never visits the island unless she thinks she can punish someone.”

“So you lied.”

“I was trying to make her look beneath the tree.”

“You said the old weather took something.”

“I thought it had. That is what my grandmother told me.”

His correction came too late to be innocent. Or saw why the copper-gloved keeper had wanted the tooth: Cale believed the flask could restore the orchard. He was mistaken. The woman on the causeway knew it, and yet she had not stopped him from bringing Or here.

Behind them, someone stepped on the causeway. Vael’s three-stitch collar glimmered in the dark. Iven followed her, carrying a paper lantern. Nemi was not with them.

“Stand away from the tree,” Vael ordered.

Cale spread his arms. “The flask is here.”

Vael looked at the exposed bottle. “That is a container for lamp oil.”

Or lifted the tide spoon. “It answers the tooth.”

“Everything answers a tool if you strike it hard enough.”

Iven took one step forward, then stopped. “There is a voice in the trunk.”

“What does it say?” Or asked.

“I don’t know. It is speaking in the sound of a closed room.”

Vael reached for the tooth. Or pulled back. The blue crown flashed in the evening, and every bell in the orchard gave a single, soft knock. The salt basin below them shivered. A seam opened in the earth, narrow as a mouth.

From somewhere under the island came a small sound: rain against a roof.

“Open it,” said Cale.

“Don’t,” said the woman with a copper hand. She had returned to the edge of the orchard. Her scarf had slipped. Or saw a crescent scar at the stranger’s temple, a mark she had once noticed on another person in the House of Folded Water, though the face attached to it had always been hidden by a hood. That hidden person’s crescent curved the opposite way; it was not the stranger’s scar.

Vael tried to seize the flask. The tree’s bark closed around it. Or slipped the blue tooth back into Nemi’s tin case and snapped it shut. Its blue had faded to gray.

A drop of water landed on Or’s wrist. It fell from the sky.

Everyone looked up. The clouds had begun to loosen.

Cale led Or around the tree’s western side, away from Vael and Iven. The oldest roots had pushed through the island’s crust and made shallow ridges in the salt. Some held tiny pockets of water, though the island was dry. Cale touched one with the copper glove. The water moved toward his hand, then withdrew.

“Those are not the cistern roots,” he said. “They are the roots the tree grew after it learned the storm’s name.”

“Can roots learn?”

“They can remember a direction. The tree has been growing toward the Red Channel for years.”

“Why didn’t anyone notice?”

“We noticed. We called it a bent tree.”

Cale explained that the Bell Orchard was not planted all at once. It had grown in uneven rows, each tree planted where a person had heard a different note. The oldest trees held the low sounds, the young trees the high ones. In spring, the copper bells usually rang in a slow sequence that taught the weather how to enter town. This year, one note had disappeared. Without it, the rain had no clear way down.

Or asked what the broken branch meant. Cale lifted it and showed her the raw end. The wood was not split; it had been bitten clean through. A small row of dents marked the cut, like teeth marks.

“The tree is shedding the part that remembers the old route,” he said. “I thought I could force it to remember by putting the branch back.”

“Would that have worked?”

“I don’t know.”

“You have been making a lot of guesses.”

“I am a keeper, not a Listener.”

He spoke without bitterness. Cale’s job was to prune, mend, and keep the orchard from growing into the salt roads. He knew where each branch rested in the wind and how many bells a tree could carry without bending. He did not know why some sounds brought rain, or why the oldest tree had been growing toward a channel nobody used.

Or showed him the blue tooth. Cale did not reach for it. He only leaned closer. The hollow in its crown seemed deeper now, and the blue had gathered at the bottom like a drop in a well.

“My grandmother called it a seed,” he said. “She said it would open when the town was ready to remember.”

“Did she say anything else?”

“She said not to let a keeper mistake custody for ownership.”

Or kept the tooth secured in Nemi’s tin case. Cale had wanted it, but not for himself. He wanted the orchard to recover. That did not make his desire harmless. The tree might have been guarding the flask from him for a reason.

They listened to the branch. Its silence was not empty. The bells gave off a minute vibration, like a word held in the throat. Or placed her ear against the cut. She heard a distant rushing sound, then a sequence of three low knocks.

Cale nodded. “That’s the same count the old jar made.”

“Perhaps the jar and the tree are listening to each other.”

“Or perhaps they are both listening to something else.”

Vael had crossed the causeway with the others and was already beside the tree; by the time the count ended, the evening wind had changed direction. The island’s salt crust trembled under their feet. Or kept Nemi’s tin case in her coat. She did not tell Cale that the woman with a copper hand had warned her not to give it to him. She wanted to know whether Cale would ask for it again, and whether he could admit why.

The seam in the earth closed before the party returned to town. It left no wet track on the causeway. The salt had sealed itself again, smooth as a sheet of pale paper. Or carried the flask’s image in her mind: green glass half-grown into the tree, the bark closing around it as if the vessel were a seed that had failed to sprout.

Vael ordered the orchard workers to count every tree and every bell. Cale objected that counting the bells would not make them ring. Vael said the count would tell them what they had lost. Cale agreed to do it, but he insisted the list include missing notes as well as missing branches. The weather clerks had no column for a note that could not be heard. Or made one in the margin.

They found nine trees with bells missing. The oldest tree had nine branches, but the drawing on the notice had only seven. Two had been added since the town began its records. Cale said the youngest branches grew toward whatever sound the older ones could no longer make. Vael said that was a poetic guess. Cale replied that it was still a better explanation than theft.

Or remained behind after the others had left. She put the tin case on a root and watched it. Nothing happened. She tapped the case once with the spoon. From inside the tree came an answering tap. She tapped twice. The tree answered with three.

The pattern made her think of a conversation where one speaker was trying not to be understood. She stopped tapping. A branch shifted above her. A dry copper bell detached and dropped into the salt. It had no clapper and no hole for one. It was simply a curved piece of copper, thin enough to bend between her fingers.

Or picked it up and put it in her pocket. Before she left, she tucked the bell into the hollow root beneath the tree, where it could remain. The tree had not asked her to take anything.

At the edge of the island, Cale showed Or the small marks he made on a strip of bark to track the orchard’s growth. Each mark indicated not a year but a change: a branch leaning west, a bell growing dull, a root seeking salt. He had kept the strip folded in his coat for years. Or asked why he had not put it in the official record. Cale said the record wanted numbers, while the trees gave him directions. He had not understood until now that directions could be more useful.

The orchard workers met beside the causeway before sunrise. They carried baskets of dull bells and strips of bark for the count. Cale asked each person to name one thing the trees had done that the official record left out. One worker described roots moving at night; another remembered a bell that rang only when no one was nearby. They made no attempt to prove the stories. They added them to the strip. Cale said a keeper had to know what the trees did, not only what could be measured.

As they left the island, Or looked back at the tree and counted its branches. There were nine, though only seven appeared in the old drawing. The two new ones leaned toward the town. She wondered whether they would keep growing that way after the storm passed, or turn back toward the salt. No one had recorded the tree’s first direction. Cale said he would begin now, and Or believed him.

Cale folded the bark strip and returned it to his coat, ready to add the next change.
