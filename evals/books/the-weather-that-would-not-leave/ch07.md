# Chapter 7: The Name Beneath the Name

The black pool collapsed at sunrise. It left a dry circle beneath the bridge and a hairline crack in every stone around it. Iven sat beside the circle, his lantern extinguished. Nemi had gone back to the dye-house. Or held the green flask, the cracked jar, the seed, and the rain vial, each in a different pocket so no one thing could touch another.

“Was that your mother’s voice?” Iven asked.

“It sounded like her.”

“Do you remember her?”

“Not well enough to know whether it was her.”

Or’s mother had died when Or was six, or so the ledger said. There was a gap in the record between the day she entered the House of Folded Water and the day her name appeared in the death column. Or had asked Vael about the gap once. Vael had said the ink was damaged.

The title Listener made Or think of Iven, who had the skill and the public trust. It also made her think of the hooded person who had walked into the Room Below Mouth every night during her first year as a clerk, leaving a different jar in the same place. Or had never seen that person’s face. At the time, she thought the visitor was a cleaner.

The rain vial began to tick. It was not an object’s ordinary sound. It was a measured sequence: three slow taps, a pause, then one quick tap. Or recognized the pattern from the jar’s hidden voice. She put the green flask beside the cracked jar. The liquid inside swam toward the glass seam.

Iven touched the flask. “It has a name.”

“What is it?”

“Not a word I know.”

“Can you listen harder?”

“I can listen longer. That is different.”

He closed his eyes. Or set the brass tide spoon between the two vessels, crescent pointing toward the Red Channel. The jar’s crack glowed. The green liquid turned once, then separated into two layers. On the lower layer, a pale mark appeared: a letter shaped like a hook.

“Is that part of a name?” Or asked.

“I think so.”

“Whose?”

“Perhaps the storm’s. Perhaps the room’s.”

They went to the House of Folded Water. In the Room Below Mouth, Or loosened the rain vial’s red thread and let one drop fall into the cracked jar, then stopped before the vial was emptied. The drops did not touch its bottom. They traveled along the inside crack and emerged as tiny blue marks. Each mark looked like a letter, but no two letters belonged to the same alphabet. Iven heard the pattern; Or saw it.

Together they described a series of turns: from the old cistern, past the room beneath the room, then west, then down. There was no stair west of the House. Or realized the directions named a route through the town’s containers, not its streets.

They went to the Drowned Stair. It was a stairwell that ended at a wall of smooth plaster. The wall had no door, but the tide spoon’s crescent fit the seam between two stones. Or pressed it there and turned. A hidden panel swung inward. Cold damp air breathed out.

Behind it, a narrow passage sloped downward. The green flask warmed in Or’s pocket. The jar showed the hook-shaped letter again, then a second mark: a circle with a cut through it.

At the passage’s end stood a small room containing nothing but a wooden chair and a spool of copper wire. A label on the chair’s underside read: FOR VEY ORIN, WHO HEARS BOTH SIDES.

Or touched the chair. Dust came away on her fingers. “She was kept here.”

“No,” said Iven. “This is where she came after she left the first chamber.”

“How do you know?”

“Because I heard her counting.”

The spool of wire rested on the chair’s seat. Or unwound one turn. Beneath it, she found a paper tag. The tag bore another name: Mara Senn. Or knew that name. It appeared in the House’s old register as an assistant who had died in a fever when Or was six.

A sound came from the passage behind them. Vael was there, carrying a folded map. “You need to leave.”

“What is Mara Senn?” Or asked.

“Your mother’s name.”

Iven looked at Vael. “Or told me you said the ink was damaged.”

“It was easier than explaining.”

“Explain now,” Or said.

Vael took the folded map from her collar. “Mara was a Listener. She was assigned to the old room after Vey was released from it. She did not die. The ledger says she died in a fever when you were six, but that was a false entry. She became the person you called mother.”

“You told me she was dead.”

“I believed the false entry.”

“Then the ledger was wrong.”

“I corrected it when I learned the truth.”

“When?”

“Yesterday.”

Or stepped back. “You’re correcting a life, not an entry.”

Vael unrolled the map. It showed the island under the Bell Orchard, with the cistern beneath it, and another chamber below that. A line of writing ran along the bottom: THE STORM WAS NAMED BY THREE LISTENERS.

“We cannot open the cistern until we know all three names,” Vael said. “The old record names Vey Orin and Mara Senn. The third name is missing.”

Or looked at the green flask. The figure inside the dark liquid had turned toward her. “Then the storm is the one that told us not to count it twice.”

“No,” Iven said quietly. “The storm told us not to count a person twice.”

The room under the room had not held one forgotten Listener. It had held the names of several people, pressed into a secret the town had mistaken for a weather problem.

Or returned to the House alone after Vael took the map. She wanted to know why her name had been written beneath the old ink. The official records were held in the Counting Room, but the archive alcove held copies of the household ledgers made for clerks. The copies were usually incomplete. She searched the index for Oruva and found no entry. She searched for Senn and found a list of households, among them her own.

The household record showed that she had been entered into the town’s register three days after her birth, without a parent listed. A note beneath it said: “Child assigned to the House under weather protection.” It did not say who assigned her. The ink was fresh compared with the rest of the page.

Or looked for Mara. The old ledger called her a dye-house worker and assistant Listener. A later copy called her deceased. There was no date, no illness, no burial place. The only detail that survived was an object: a red-thread vial transferred from Mara to “the third ear.” Or realized the vial had been the one Nemi gave her. Nemi had not known what it contained, but it had belonged to her mother.

She found another entry for Vey. Her name appeared in several forms: Vey Orin, Vey of the Orchard, First Listener. In the oldest entry, someone had written “the copper-handed woman” in the margin. Or had seen all those references and assumed they described different people. She copied the names into her notebook, then crossed out “First Listener” because it was a role, not a separate person.

A knock sounded behind her. Tavin stood in the archive doorway with a stack of empty jars. He had heard about the inspection and wanted to know whether the rain would be released.

“I don’t know,” Or said.

“Do you think the town will flood?”

“I don’t know.”

“Do you think Vael is lying?”

“Sometimes.”

“Do you think I should report that you were in the archive?”

“Would you?”

He set the jars down. “I think reports are supposed to help the person reading them. The person reading mine is Vael, and she already knows everything I know.”

Or showed him the old entries. Tavin read slowly, moving his lips. He recognized her name and Mara’s, then checked the date. “You were born under stored weather.”

“I was born during a storm, apparently.”

“That is a strange thing to put in a ledger.”

“Maybe a person’s life is part of the weather.”

He seemed unhappy with that answer. He preferred rules that could be followed, categories that stayed put. Or understood. She had used to think the ledgers were more reliable than people. Now she could see how carefully the ledger had been made to agree with the people who held it.

Tavin returned his jar—the one with a crack in its base—to the shelf. It had a crack in its base, tiny but visible. He said he had found it among the empty ones. Or marked it with chalk and set it aside. The jar was not part of the mystery, as far as she could tell. But she had begun to understand that an ordinary vessel could still have a history, and that a history could be hidden without anyone intending to hide it.

She left the archive with a copy of the name list. Behind her, Tavin remained with the jars, counting them again from the beginning.

The next day, Or and Vael walked to the market court to inspect the paving. The stones had been replaced in sections over many years. Old blocks sat beneath new ones, and the line between them made a shallow spiral. Vael said the spiral was only a result of the masons’ work. Or followed its curve to the western edge, where one stone sat slightly lower than the rest.

They lifted it together. Beneath lay a black glass disk with a hole in the center. The hole matched the base of the old jar. Vael held the disk, while Or held the jar. The crack in its side aligned with the hole. A dry gust passed through the glass and stirred the dust on the road.

Vael put the stone back. “We should not dismantle the court without a plan.”

“Do you believe the path is under it?”

“I believe the map points there. I believe the roof tiles agree. I believe the jars moved toward it.”

“Do you believe the town should open it?”

“I don’t know.”

For Vael, the answer was a confession. She had spent years making decisions from the ledger, then treating the ledger as if it had decided for her. Or could see the burden in her posture, the way her shoulders had rounded toward the three-stitch collar.

They went to the public hall. The weather allotments were nearly gone. Vael told the households they would receive no rain that week. She did not say the orchard had stolen it. She did not say the sky was empty. She said the town had lost the old route and needed to find it again.

Some people complained. One baker asked whether she should keep her ovens hot. A child asked whether rain could get lost. Vael answered both questions honestly: she did not know, and perhaps.

Or stood beside her. She recognized the importance of the answer. The town had been given a false certainty for years, and fear had grown around it. A little uncertainty would not harm them as much as another lie.

When they left, the child gave Or a paper boat. It was folded from the old posted orchard notice, with the words “THE BELL ORCHARD IS OUT OF TUNE” visible along its side. Or set it in the dry channel. The boat did not move, but the waterline beside it rose a fraction.

Or kept the name list tucked inside her coat. She had not decided whether to tell Tavin what she had found. He was careful with records, and she trusted him to understand the difference between a missing entry and a missing person. But the list included her own name among the Listeners, and she was not yet sure she wanted anyone else to see it. She folded the paper once more and placed it on the table in her room. Neither the paper nor the room made a sound.

Tavin came to the House carrying his jar with a crack in its base, which he had found in the public hall. He said it had been on the shelf, not on the sill, when he began his shift. Or asked whether he had moved it. He said he had not. She showed him the small blue crescent reflected in its base. He looked at it, then at the door to the lower room. For once, he did not ask for an explanation. He set the jar beside the door and went back to his inventory.

Or took the copied names to the roof and compared them with the green line in the tiles. The line bent west at the place the listening road left the House. She wrote “listening road” in the margin. The phrase was not in the town’s official record, but it described what they had found better than any measurement. She folded the paper around the map and put both in her coat.

The name list stayed folded in her coat, close enough to feel but not yet to read.
