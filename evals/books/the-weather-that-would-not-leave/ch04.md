# Chapter 4: The First Drop

The drop that had landed on Or’s wrist did not roll. It stayed where it landed, trembling like a bead of clear glass. Vael reached for it with the blue bowl from the Counting Room, but the drop rose from Or’s skin and vanished into the night. The bowl was empty again; it had not caught the tooth, which remained in Nemi’s tin case.

The clouds had not opened. They had only drawn apart, leaving a seam across the sky. A line of pale light showed through, then was covered again. The bells in the orchard made no sound.

“Everyone back to the town,” Vael said. “The island is unsafe.”

Cale stayed beside the tree. “The flask is under the bark.”

“Then the tree may keep it.”

Or watched Iven. “You heard something.”

“I heard a room,” he said. “There was a door, and someone on the other side was counting.”

“How many?”

“Not numbers.” He shook his head. “Drops.”

The woman with a copper hand stood behind the keeper, her face turned away. Or caught the crescent scar again. The scar pulled the skin above the stranger’s left eye, the same way it did on the portrait of the first Listener that hung in the Counting Room. Or had never seen that portrait clearly; it had been painted over when she was very young.

She reached toward the stranger. “Wait. What is your name?”

The woman touched the copper plates on her fingers. “Vey.”

“Vey what?”

“Just Vey.” She looked at Or then, and for a moment her face seemed familiar. “I was the other Listener. I heard the storm before I understood it.”

Iven lowered the lantern. “You were gone.”

“I was folded away.”

Vael stepped between them. “That is not a name in the town ledger.”

“It is a name in the first one.” Vey glanced at the tooth. “The flask holds the part of the storm the tree could not digest. The tooth opens it. The tide spoon can turn it.”

The brass crescent warmed in Or’s hand. “Turn it where?”

“Toward the place the rain remembers.”

Vael took the tin case from Or and removed the tooth. This time it did not resist her. “We will go to the House. This object will be locked in the Counting Room.”

Cale grabbed Vael’s wrist. “You don’t know what you’re doing.”

“I know that a town cannot survive on mysteries.”

He released her. “A town can’t survive on dry roofs either.”

Vael put the tooth inside the blue bowl and covered it with her ledger. Then she handed the empty tin case back to Or. Or noticed that the tooth no longer clicked. It had given its sound to the fallen drop.

At the causeway, rain began to fall in a circle around the orchard. Each drop landed on salt and disappeared. The island remained dry. As the group crossed back, Or saw a small green thread in the seam of her sleeve. The thread was wet. She pulled it free, and a single blue bead rolled from its knot. It had been caught in the loose stitching of Or’s sleeve, not in Nemi’s case.

She hid the bead in her fist. It was round as a seed, with a dark line through its middle.

Vael saw the movement. “What did you find?”

“Nothing.”

“Nothing makes a very crowded pocket.”

“Ask Nemi.”

“I will.”

In town, Vael led the tooth into the Counting Room and placed it in the blue bowl beneath a glass lid. Iven remained with it. Or was ordered to return to her post in the House of Folded Water. Cale was sent back to the Bell Orchard under guard. The woman called Vey walked beside Or for half a street, then slipped down an alley without making a sound.

“I know you,” Or said.

“Not yet.”

“Where did I see the scar?”

Vey tapped her temple. “You saw it before you could read.”

Then she was gone.

Or went to the House by the upper route, passing the Silt Choir and a shuttered stairwell called the Drowned Stair. The rain’s circle had followed her, but it did not touch the stones. A blue bead of water clung to the brass tide spoon. The spoon’s handle felt warm, almost like skin.

In the Room Below Mouth, she placed the bead beside the jar that had sung. The jar did not ring this time. Instead, a faint line appeared in its glaze, running from lip to base. Or pressed her ear against it.

A voice inside said, “Count the names, not the drops.”

She drew back. The Room Below Mouth was empty, all its shelves orderly and dry. Yet she had heard the sentence clearly, and she knew the voice was not hers.

That night Vael put up her lantern notice at the Silt Choir. The inspection of the Bell Orchard had found no stolen water. The town would continue rationing rain until further notice. The first drop, the notice said, had been a trick of the lanterns.

Or read it and understood that Vael had lied. But she did not yet know whether Vael had lied to protect the town, or to keep it afraid.

At the House, Or washed the blue bead in a basin. It did not dissolve. The water around it clouded, as if a pinch of ash had been stirred through. She held the bead between finger and thumb and watched its dark line slowly rotate. The little bead was a fragment of something larger, perhaps a mark, perhaps a seed. Its line pointed toward the western corridor.

She carried it to the old floor panel. The black groove beneath the shelf had closed, but the bead fit into the round indentation on the stone. When she set it there, the panel warmed. A shallow pattern appeared: a bowl, a branch, a channel. The shapes were connected by fine blue lines. The tide spoon had shown a direction before; now the bead showed a route.

Or fetched a scrap of ledger paper and copied the pattern. The drawing was not a street map. It showed the House as a series of nested circles, with the Room Below Mouth at the center and the hidden hollow beneath the first shelf below it. A blue line ran from the hollow outward to the Bell Orchard, then doubled back beneath the House. The route appeared to circle itself.

She left the bead on the stone. By the time she returned with a light, it was gone. The groove was dry. Nothing in the room had moved, but the jar with the crack had shifted to the edge of its shelf. Or caught it before it fell.

She set the vessel down. A faint warmth passed into her palms. This time she heard no sentence. She heard a sound like a person walking through a room in wet shoes. The steps began at one end, stopped in the middle, and resumed beyond the door.

Or opened the jar. It was empty except for a smear of blue at the base. The water had gone somewhere, but not through the crack. The jar’s rim was sealed. She marked its position on a strip of chalk, then set it back on the shelf. If she returned later and found it moved, she would know the room had done it.

The next morning, the chalk mark was broken in two. The jar stood a finger’s width to the left. Or did not tell Vael. She took the blue bowl from the Counting Room and placed it beside the vessel. The bowl remained perfectly still. When she set the brass tide spoon across both rims, the spoon tilted toward the jar.

It was a small experiment, and it told her little. But she learned that the spoon recognized the jar, even when no water was present. She also learned that the Room Below Mouth had a current, like a river passing through walls. The current did not move objects unless they already had somewhere to go.

At lunch, Or went to the Silt Choir and watched the vendors pour their remaining allotments into sample cups. One vendor poured a spoonful of fog into a shallow dish. It rose, drifted around the stall, and settled on a sleeping child’s hair. The mother laughed and thanked the seller. A moment of weather was worth more to her than the price of the dish.

Or thought about the town’s measurement customs. Rain was weighed, fog was folded, wind was tied into paper strips. Each weather had its own vessel and its own rule. No one asked whether weather wanted to be measured. The jars had always seemed like shelves for something useful. Now they seemed like rooms whose doors had been shut from the outside.

That night, the weather clerks gathered in the upper hall to debate the notice. Vael said the public needed a clear statement. Iven said clarity was no excuse for a false one. The two clerks spoke in turns, without raising their voices, while the others watched the rainless windows.

Or sat on the stairs and listened. She heard Vael describe the town as a row of jars that could not all be opened at once. She heard Iven say that a jar was only useful if its contents could leave. Neither could persuade the other. Their words returned to the same argument, like rain running in a circle beneath the island.

When the meeting ended, Vael found Or on the stair. She asked whether she had heard the jar again. Or said yes. Vael asked what it had said. Or repeated the sentence about counting names, not drops. Vael did not laugh or call it nonsense. She asked whether the voice sounded like a person.

“I can’t tell,” Or said.

“Could it have been your mother?”

Or wanted to say no. Instead, she said she did not know. Vael nodded, as if uncertainty were a fact she could file.

She returned the blue bowl to the Counting Room. Inside its bottom was a thin line of water. Vael touched it with one finger and smelled the water. “This came from the orchard,” she said. “It smells like copper.”

“Does that prove the tree made it?”

“No. It proves I was wrong to say there was no water.”

Vael wiped her finger on a cloth. She had not yet corrected the public notice. Or could see she was preparing to, and also that she feared the correction would make the town less obedient. The first truth had been small, a drop on a wrist. The lie around it had grown much larger.

On the way back to the House, Or crossed the Silt Choir while the vendors were packing up. A seller offered her a jar of “yesterday’s mist,” an allotment no one had claimed. She declined. The seller poured the mist into a bowl and let it fade. Or watched it vanish without being measured, and wondered whether the town had been afraid to waste weather because it believed there would never be more. The rain circling the orchard had proved that the sky could still make its own choices.

Or wrote the sentence from the jar on a fresh page and placed it in her pocket. She did not add a date. It seemed wrong to make the voice belong to one particular morning. Later, when the paper grew damp, the ink spread but the words remained legible. She put it between the pages of her notebook rather than in the official ledger. The sentence had not asked to be certified.

Before she slept, Or checked the jar’s shelf one last time. The vessel stood where she had left it. Its crack was dark and still. She held the tide spoon beside it, not touching the clay, and felt no warmth. She put the spoon away. The first drop had changed the town, but the room had not become a place of constant wonders. It remained mostly shelves, dust, and careful work.

The blue bowl held no water. The drop’s path was gone, but its coldness remained on Vael’s finger.
