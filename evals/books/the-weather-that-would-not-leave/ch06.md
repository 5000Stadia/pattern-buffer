# Chapter 6: What Nemi Kept

Nemi Tallow was waiting in the old dye-house, where the drying racks hung empty and the walls wore a permanent red blush. She had lit no lamp. Or found her by following the little blue clicks in the dark.

“You gave me the case,” Or said.

“I gave you a container. It came back empty.”

“You said the tooth belonged to a tree.”

“It did.”

“You said the tree flask held the rest of the storm.”

“I said it held something the tree couldn’t digest. I never said it was the rest.”

Or sat on a workbench. “I found the room under the room.”

Nemi stopped moving. “Who showed you?”

“Vael.”

“Then she knows you know.”

“She said there was no bargain.”

Nemi made a sound that might have been a laugh. “Vael’s first mother signed it.”

“What did the town give the storm?”

“A name. A place. A promise to let it pass through us once every eleven years. The town kept the first two and broke the last.”

“Why would anyone do that?”

“Because storms are difficult guests. They arrive with a great deal of water and leave your walls somewhere else.”

“Then why bring this one back?”

“I’m not bringing anything back.” Nemi reached under the bench and pulled out a narrow metal box with six sliding lids. She opened the first lid. Inside was a folded sheet of blue paper. Under the second, a tiny spool of thread. The third held a pinch of white salt. She opened the fourth compartment, then the fifth. The sixth lid stuck.

Or leaned forward. “What is in it?”

“Something I haven’t told anyone.”

The stuck lid came free. Inside lay a small black seed, no larger than a tear. A line of light ran along its shell.

Nemi said, “When Mara was put below the House, I was the child who carried the key.”

“You were there?”

“I was a child. I was told it was a game.”

“You remember her?”

“I remember someone with a copper hand, though I did not know her name.”

Or looked at the seed. “What is this?”

“A seed from the bell tree. It will grow only where a name has been erased.”

“Why did you keep it?”

“Because I didn’t know what else to do with the shame.”

The old dye-house had three doors, all painted the same red as the walls. From the nearest, a breeze moved through the room, bringing with it the scent of fresh rain. Nemi put the seed in Or’s palm. It was warm.

“You have to find the missing part of the storm’s name,” she said. “It’s in the jar.”

“The jar is cracked.”

“The jar is a map.”

Or closed her fingers. “And the blue tooth?”

“An opening word.”

“The tree flask?”

“A mouth.”

“The tide spoon?”

“A direction.”

“And the seed?”

“A witness.”

Nemi took out a small glass vial, sealed with red thread, and pressed it into Or’s hand. “This vial belonged to my mother. I don’t know what she kept in it. If you pour it into the jar, it may show you where the storm’s name went.”

“Why didn’t you tell me any of this earlier?”

“Because I did not know you could hear the jar.”

“You knew I had the spoon.”

“I knew it had been your mother’s. I didn’t know what she had put inside, or that it had chosen you.”

The vial was cool and so clear that its contents were hard to see. Or slipped it into the inner pocket of her coat. She put the seed in the tin case. Nemi’s six-lidded box remained open on the bench, its compartments displayed like a small map of hidden things.

A bell rang somewhere outside. The dye-house had no bells. Nemi closed the box and placed it under the bench.

“Someone is calling from the canal,” she said.

They went to the Red Channel, a narrow waterway that ran through the western side of town. It was dry except for a single black pool beneath a stone bridge. A figure stood at the bridge’s far end. Iven Orri held a green glass flask in both hands.

“I found it,” he said. “It was in the wall behind the scratched portrait in the Counting Room.”

The flask was not the one beneath the tree. This one had a white stopper and a seam of yellow glass. A smell of wet bark came through it.

Or stared. “Where did it come from?”

“I don’t know. It was hidden before I was made Listener.”

Nemi touched the bridge’s stones. “That’s not a storm vessel.”

“What is it?” Or asked.

“Someone’s answer.”

Iven gave the flask to Or. “I can’t hear what it contains.”

Or’s fingers brushed the glass. In the dark liquid within, a pale shape turned slowly, like a fish looking for a door. The flask’s hidden contents had been waiting in the wall all this time. She had no idea what it was, but she knew it was listening.

A second bell rang from beyond the bridge. Iven turned. The black pool expanded without spilling, its edge unfolding like cloth. Then a voice spoke from inside it, using Or’s mother’s voice:

“Do not let them count me twice.”

The black pool beneath the bridge shrank after the voice spoke. Or placed the green flask on the dry stones and waited for the shape inside to settle. It did not. The pale form remained in motion, turning in the dark fluid. Iven had heard the voice but would not say whether it was Or’s mother or someone who had borrowed her voice.

Nemi walked along the bridge, looking at the waterline etched into the stone. There were marks every few inches, each cut at a different angle. The oldest mark sat above their heads. A newer one crossed it at knee height, though no rain had filled the channel since Or’s childhood.

“The channel used to rise this far,” Or said.

“It used to rise higher,” Nemi replied. “The market court has three layers of paving. Every time the town covered the market basin, it lowered the waterline on the wall.”

“Why cover it?”

“People wanted a place to trade. The basin was inconvenient.”

“Was the storm supposed to pass through the market basin?”

“It was supposed to pass through the town. The market basin was just where it left.”

Nemi showed Or the six compartments of her box again, explaining the objects she had hidden there. The folded blue paper was a receipt from the old bargain. The spool of thread marked the path from the House to the channel. The pinch of salt came from the first storm’s island. A small copper ring was tucked beneath the fifth lid. Nemi said she had put it there after finding it in the carved-names chamber as a child. The sixth compartment had held the black seed.

“Why keep the ring?” Or asked.

“It belonged to Vey. It was cut from the old bell tree and worn by the first Listener. I found it in the chamber when I was a child.”

“Did you give it back?”

“I did not know who it belonged to.”

“You know now.”

Nemi held the ring out. Or took it to the edge of the bridge. Vey was not there, but the ring warmed in Or’s hand. She placed it on the stone and stepped away. The ring remained where she put it. No hidden current carried it off. In the morning, the ring was gone; Vey had taken it from the bridge.

Then, from inside the green flask, a low note sounded. The pale shape stopped turning. It had become a narrow black line, pointing directly down the Red Channel. Iven bent close and listened. “It isn’t a voice,” he said. “It’s the place where a voice will be.”

Or wondered whether the jar under the House and the flask in her hand were parts of the same sentence. The jar had told her to count names, not drops. The flask now pointed to a direction. Nemi had given her a seed, a vial, and a route made from objects. Each thing held part of a message, but no one had written it down as words.

They went to the old dye-house. Behind its racks, Or found a painted line along the wall. The red pigment was the color of dried blood, but it was made from clay and flower husks. A narrow arrow pointed west. The arrow had been covered by a rack for years.

Nemi touched the wall. “My mother painted that.” Nemi’s mother was a different dye-house worker from Mara.

“Did she know the bargain?”

“She knew that something had to pass through. She did not know what it was.”

Or drew a line in her notebook, copying the arrow. For the first time, the town’s old objects did not seem like clues left by clever people. They seemed like pieces of a path, left by ordinary people who did not have the whole map.

Or took the green flask to the House and placed it in the old archive alcove. She wanted to see whether it would respond to the weather calendars. The first book remained open at the year forty-one, its note “seal anyway” visible beside the official entry. The flask’s black line leaned toward that page.

She set the bottle on the ledger. The ink around the margin softened, though the page remained dry. A second line surfaced beneath the note: “The route was closed at the west court.” It had been written in a hand unlike the clerk’s, with each letter angled as if the writer had been leaning over a moving boat.

Or copied the words. When she lifted the flask, the line faded. She replaced it and saw another sentence: “Do not name the water for what it carries.” The old weather records had always given each rain a purpose, a quantity, and a place. Perhaps that was what the sentence warned against. If the storm carried a memory, the town had labeled it as rain and forgotten the rest.

Iven arrived. He said the book was an unreliable witness. Or asked whether he considered a person reliable. He answered that a witness could be honest and still misunderstand what they saw. She took that to mean the ledger was like the people who wrote it: useful, partial, and capable of being wrong.

They returned the book to its shelf. Or put the green flask in a padded cloth to keep it from breaking. She did not seal the cloth. A container should not become another locked room by accident.

Nemi waited with Or until the first light reached the bridge. They watched the black pool dry from the edges inward. It left a narrow ring of blue on the stone, the same color as the wax from the jar’s seal. Nemi scraped a little of it into the tin case. She said she wanted to remember what the pool had looked like before anyone named it. Or asked whether memory could survive without a name. Nemi answered that she did not know, but she wanted to try.

At midday, the vendors of the Silt Choir began to sing again, but their bargains were tentative. One offered a jar of quiet, another a measure of wind. A woman had brought a cup of fog and asked whether anyone remembered what it was for. No one did. Nemi took the cup and poured the fog into the open air. It drifted above the market, then thinned. “Sometimes the use is to let it go,” she said. No one disputed her.

In the morning, a faint round impression remained in the dust where Or had left the copper ring. Nemi said someone had taken it. Or thought the channel might have carried it away. They did not search. The ring had already moved through enough hands to prove that it could be lost without disappearing from the story.

The blue ring on the stone slowly dried. No one claimed it as proof of anything.

In the days that followed, Or returned to the Red Channel at different hours. At noon, it was only stone and shadow. At dusk, it smelled faintly of wet bark. Once, she heard a voice from beneath the bridge, but it said no words she could recognize. She stopped trying to make every sound into a message. Listening, she learned, could include letting a thing remain unclear.
