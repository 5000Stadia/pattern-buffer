# Chapter 5: The Room Under the Room

In the morning, Or found the jar had split along its new line. The crack was too narrow to leak, yet when she breathed near it, the air inside smelled of rain and stone. She took the jar upstairs, hid it in a flour sack, and walked to the Counting Room with the brass tide spoon under her coat.

Iven was alone. The tooth remained beneath the glass lid, gray and unresponsive. The blue bowl was on the table. The ledger Vael had used to cover the tooth was gone.

“Have you heard the room again?” Or asked.

“I have heard it all night.”

“What does it count?”

“People who are still waiting.”

“That sounds like a thing you can count.”

“Not with numbers.”

Iven lifted the lid. The tooth lay on a square of wool. He held it in his fingers. “It was warm when Vael brought it. Now it is cold.”

“Give it to me.”

“No.” He set the tooth down. “You’re asking the wrong question. The tooth is a word. The tree flask is a mouth. What is the sentence?”

Or laid the brass tide spoon beside the bowl. Its handle curved toward the tooth. “The jar said to count the names, not the drops.”

Iven’s fingers tightened. “The first Listener said that.”

“Who was she?”

He looked at the scratched portrait hanging above the door. A faint crescent scar showed under the ragged paint. “Her name was Vey Orin.”

Or felt the floor tilt slightly. “The woman on the causeway?”

“Perhaps.”

“She said she was the other Listener.”

“She was. Until the town decided there could be only one.”

“Why was she folded away?”

“Because she heard a storm we did not want to hear.” Iven set the tooth back down. “The first storm arrived before Ors had houses. It had a name. Vey heard it. She told the town the storm was not weather but a witness. The clerks sealed it under the island, and the people who could hear it were forbidden to speak its name.”

“Why?”

“Because if the storm was a witness, then it had seen the town’s first bargain.”

A knock sounded at the door. Vael entered with a pair of guards, both carrying the hooked poles used to lift sealed jars. “Or, you are to come with me.”

“What happened?”

“Someone entered the Room Below Mouth last night.”

“I was there.”

“After you left.” Vael’s glance fell to the spoon. “A vessel is missing.”

“Which one?”

“The oldest jar. First North Drizzle.”

Or had hidden it in the flour sack. Her mouth went dry. She had not taken the jar from the House; she had moved it inside the House, from the lower room to her sleeping alcove. Still, Vael was right: the vessel was missing from its shelf.

“I don’t know where it is.”

Vael studied her. “You are a poor liar.”

“I’m an honest clerk.”

“Those are not the same thing.”

Iven stood. “She has not left the House.”

“Then the jar is in the House.”

“Which contains many rooms.”

“Only one room below the stairs.” Vael turned to the guards. “Search her quarters.”

Or thought of the flour sack. If they found the jar, they would see the crack. She could tell the truth and explain the voice. She could also tell them Vey had returned, and Iven had confirmed her name. But Vael already believed the orchard had taken the rain, and the lie had made her certain enough to send guards after a jar that had never left the building.

“Wait,” Or said. “I moved it.”

The guards went to her room. Vael followed. Or showed her the flour sack, and the jar within it. The clerk did not look angry. She looked relieved, which was worse.

“You heard something,” Vael said. “And you kept it from me.”

“I heard a voice.”

“Whose?”

“I don’t know.”

“Then why keep the jar?”

“Because it was the first thing in years to make a sound.”

Vael lifted the sack. The jar’s crack shone a thin blue. She did not touch it. “The room below the House is not the lowest room.”

“There’s another?”

“Not anymore.” Vael carried the sack toward the stairwell. “We built a room over it.”

The guards had not known. Iven had not known. Or knew because Vael had said it, and could see from her face that she regretted saying it.

At the end of the House’s western corridor was a disused cistern door. Vael unlocked it with a black iron key. Behind the door, a staircase descended into a room that smelled of cold clay. A circular hollow marked its center. It was dry and empty, but the walls were carved with hundreds of names.

Or found Vey Orin among them. The letters had been cut in a hand that looked like hers.

“This was where they put the first Listener,” Vael said. “She was not killed. She was erased from the town’s records.”

“Then she escaped.”

“Or someone released her.”

On the wall beside the name, Or found a sentence: THE STORM OWES NO ONE ITS MEMORY.

Vael touched the carving. “That is why we don’t speak of the bargain.”

“What bargain?”

She took her hand away. “There was a bargain, but no one in this House remembers its exact words.”

Or turned to her. “Then why pretend there was no bargain?”

“I did not say there was no bargain. I said I did not know its exact words.”

The tide spoon shifted in Or’s sleeve. In the dry room, the metal sounded like a drop of water hitting a bowl.

After Vael left the carved-names chamber, Or stayed beside the carved names. She traced the letters of Vey Orin, careful not to put her fingernail into the cuts. The stone had been smoothed by touch around the name. Someone had come here often, perhaps to check that it remained, perhaps to assure themselves it was still erased from the official ledger.

She asked Iven whether he remembered the room. He did not. “I was not the Listener then,” he said. “I was a child in the upper floors. I remember the day they painted over the portrait when I was a child. I remember the smell of wet plaster. I do not remember who ordered it.”

“Do you think Vey was dangerous?”

“I think she heard something the clerks could not control.”

“Is that the same thing?”

“To a clerk, often.”

They examined the wall marks. The names were arranged in three rings. The outer ring held roles: Listener, Keeper, Witness, Holder. The inner ring contained family names. The center ring was blank. Or found a small groove in its middle, shaped like the bowl of the brass spoon.

She pressed the crescent against the stone. Nothing happened. Iven placed his palm beside hers. The groove grew warm, and a thin line of water ran out from the wall. It crossed the name Vey Orin, then continued to the line THE STORM OWES NO ONE ITS MEMORY. The sentence darkened as the water passed over it.

“Someone wrote this after the chamber was closed,” Or said.

“Or the wall wrote it itself.”

“That is not an explanation.”

“It is what I heard.”

The stream stopped at the carved words. A bead of water gathered beneath “no one.” The meaning seemed plain, though neither was certain: the storm’s memory did not belong to the town, the clerks, the orchard, or the people who had stored it. Or wiped the wall with her sleeve. The letters remained damp.

Vael returned carrying an empty jar. She said it had been found behind the ledgers in the Counting Room. Its label had been scratched away, leaving only a strip of black wax. Or recognized the wax as the kind used to seal old weather vessels. The jar had no visible crack. When she set it near the wall, the damp letters lifted from the stone and appeared in its glaze.

Vael stepped back. “That is not possible.”

“Everything in this room seems to be doing it.”

“Do not write this down.”

“Why?”

“Because a record can become an order.”

Or asked whether the jar had been hidden by Vey. Vael said she did not know. She was honest for once, and the answer made Or trust her a fraction more than a lie would have.

The three of them carried the nameless jar out of the carved-names chamber and up to the House’s upper floor. They did not take it to Low Weather or the Counting Room. Vael put it on a table beside the House ledger, where the town’s names were recorded. By morning, the blank label had acquired a single line of blue lettering. It was not a name. It was a road, drawn in miniature, bending from the House toward the west.

Or spent the following morning copying the names from the wall onto separate slips of paper. She arranged them in the same three rings and tried different combinations. Vey and Mara appeared together in every version. The other names did not. Some of the names had been cut so deeply that the stone around them had cracked.

Iven came in carrying an old wooden stool. He said it had belonged to the first Listener, though he could not remember how he knew. The stool’s legs were uneven. On its underside, the same hooked mark appeared as the one on the tree flask Or had seen beneath the orchard bark. Or set the stool beside the empty jar. The jar’s glaze showed the word “west.”

“Someone made the room into a map,” she said.

“Someone needed a way through it.”

They looked for a door in the carved walls. There was none. But the black iron key fit a shallow notch beneath the stool. Vael still carried the key, so they did not try to open the notch. Iven said not to force anything that had waited this long.

Or returned the slips to the wall, placing them over their corresponding names. The paper names aligned with the carved ones, but the final slip had no match. She had written “Oruva Senn” on it, then left it face down. The blank center ring waited beneath her hand.

The old jar made a soft click. Or lifted the paper. The blank had developed a thin blue curve, like the beginning of a letter. She did not try to complete it. She placed the paper back and waited for the mark to fade.

It did not. Instead, the blue curve moved to the edge of the page, where it joined the line drawn on the nameless jar’s label. The objects were tracing the same route by different means. Or folded the paper and tucked it into her sleeve.

Before leaving the chamber, Vael asked Or to help her close the panel. They lowered it together, then stood in the corridor without speaking. Vael said the old room had been built before her office existed. Or asked whether she feared what else the walls might reveal. Vael said she feared that the town would use each discovery to excuse the next one. Or answered that hiding a discovery could do the same. Vael looked at her for a long moment, then left the panel unlocked.

When Or returned to her alcove, she put the flour sack back beneath the mattress. It was empty now, but she could still smell the jar’s cold clay. She had once used the sack to carry bread from the Silt Choir and had forgotten it there. Ordinary objects had a way of collecting histories without anyone keeping track. She decided not to wash it. If she ever needed to remember the first jar, the smell might be enough.

Vael closed the chamber door, removed the key from its lock, and fastened it inside her collar. She said the passage would be recorded as part of the House, not hidden beneath it. Or asked whether the public would be allowed inside. Vael said they would decide after the town understood what the room was for. Or did not argue. For the first time, the answer sounded like a decision still being made rather than a rule already set.

Or let the panel rest ajar. It no longer concealed the names from anyone willing to look.
