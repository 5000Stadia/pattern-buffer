# Chapter 1: The Weather That Would Not Leave

In Ors, rain did not fall. It arrived by appointment.

At the beginning of each thaw, the town’s weather clerks opened the high cisterns and let a measured quantity of rain descend through the roofs. Each household received its allotment in a named jar: three days of soft rain for the bakers, two hours of hard rain for the roof-menders, a little fog for the children who had never seen a hill. Weather was kept like flour. It could be borrowed, divided, or spoiled. The clouds above Ors were pale and empty, and the town’s roofs had been dry for so long that they rang when birds landed.

Oruva Senn, who was called Or by everyone except the clerks, worked beneath the town in the House of Folded Water. She knew the exact sound of an empty jar. It was a small, bright complaint, like a spoon tapping the bottom of a cup.

At dawn she stood in the Room Below Mouth with a wick-lamp in one hand and a brass tide spoon in the other. The room was a round chamber under the House, lined with shelves that held vessels no wider than a thumb and no larger than a sleeping dog. Each vessel bore a number and a weather-name. The brass tide spoon had been her mother’s, and its handle ended in a narrow crescent that fit neatly between Or’s fingers. She used it to lift drops from the measuring basins, though the clerks said a spoon could not measure weather.

“Then why does it know when the jars are lying?” she had once asked.

They had laughed and stopped asking her to explain.

The jar on the lowest shelf rang.

Or touched it with the spoon. A second sound answered from somewhere inside the clay: a faint patter, though the jar had been sealed for eleven years. Its label read FIRST NORTH DRIZZLE, YEAR 41. No north wind had been recorded in that year. Or had copied the old book herself.

She lifted the jar. It was cold enough to sting her palm.

“Leave it,” said Tavin Rell from the doorway.

Tavin was the junior clerk, a narrow boy with a face that seemed always to be apologizing. He was not named in the inventory, though he wanted to be. He had been appointed to count jars, not open them.

“It’s raining inside,” Or said.

“Rain can’t be inside.”

“Then the jar is making the sound for its own amusement.”

Tavin took a step in. “The chief clerk said no one touches the first shelf.”

“The chief clerk says a great many things.”

“Not this week. The Bell Orchard has lost a branch.”

That made Or look up. The Bell Orchard stood on a low island east of town, where the trees grew copper bells instead of fruit. Their ringing called the town to its weather allotment. If a branch had broken, the next opening would be delayed. Ors had three weeks of stored rain left, perhaps less.

She set down the jar, but the tapping did not stop. She took a little wax from the lamp dish and pressed it into the jar’s seal. The wax went dark blue. It had not been blue a moment before.

Someone had put a blue tooth inside.

Or looked at Tavin. “Did you see?”

“I saw wax.”

She kept the jar under her apron and carried it upstairs. At the top of the stair, the House’s public doors opened onto the Silt Choir, a long covered market where people sang their bargains to keep them from drying out. The vendors had begun their morning pitches. “Two handfuls of fog, one for a copper!” called a woman with a wicker basket. “Wind for the east stairs, wind for the east stairs!” cried another.

Or crossed the Choir to the counter of Nemi Tallow, who sold empty containers and kept secrets in them. Nemi was broad-shouldered, warm-voiced, and never in a hurry to learn a thing she could charge for later.

“You’re carrying a jar,” Nemi said.

“I’m carrying nothing.”

“Then it’s a very conspicuous nothing.”

Or set the jar on the counter. “Can you open this without breaking the seal?”

Nemi’s smile disappeared. “Where did you get it?”

“Under the House.”

“You should put it back.”

“Why?”

“Because there are seals that keep weather in, and seals that keep it from remembering the way out.”

Nemi took a small knife from her sleeve. The blade had been sharpened so often it was almost a needle. She slid it under the wax. It came away whole. The jar’s clay lip gave a tiny sigh. From within came the smell of wet stone, a scent Or had only known from the stories of the old river.

Nemi tipped the jar. A blue tooth fell into her palm.

It was not a tooth from any animal Or knew. It had a soft ridged root and a crown shaped like a closed eye. The tooth shivered when it touched air.

“Don’t put it near a window,” Nemi said.

“Why?”

“Because it will call what it remembers.”

A shadow fell across the counter. Cale Rusk, the keeper of the Bell Orchard, had come through the market with a broken branch under one arm. He wore a copper glove on his right hand, its fingers bent into permanent hooks from pruning bellwood. People called him Cale, though the orchard workers called him Keeper.

“Is that the tooth?” he asked.

Nemi closed her hand over it. “No.”

Cale looked at Or. “If it is, give it to me.”

Or had not told him what she found. She moved the tide spoon into her left hand and felt its crescent bite her palm. “Why?”

“Because it belongs in the orchard.”

“Everything belongs somewhere,” Nemi said. “That doesn’t mean you get to decide where.”

Cale lowered the branch. Its copper bells were silent. “The orchard has begun to forget the sound of rain. I have to return what was taken.”

“Taken by whom?” Or asked.

“By the old weather.”

This was the first time she heard anyone say that weather could take something. She looked at the blue tooth, still trembling beneath Nemi’s fingers. The jar on the counter was empty now, but a drop formed at its lip and fell upward.

Cale’s copper glove twitched. “Give me the tooth.”

Nemi slid it into the lining of her apron instead. “No.”

Or did not yet know what the tooth was, or why Cale had recognized it. She knew only that eleven years of silence had ended in a room beneath her feet, and that the first drop of rain in Ors had chosen to climb instead of fall.

That evening, the weather clerks announced that the next allotment would be canceled. A notice was nailed to the wall at the Silt Choir: THE BELL ORCHARD IS OUT OF TUNE. DO NOT OPEN THE CISTERNS.

Or read the posted notice twice. The second time, water darkened the paper from behind.

The clerks’ notice was printed on a sheet of pressed reed, and Or knew the watermark. It belonged to the yearly inventory, not to the weather office. Whoever had written on it had taken the page from a bundle kept in the House’s northern cabinet. She turned it over. The reverse side showed the month marks, one for each allotment, and a little drawing of the Bell Orchard’s oldest tree. The tree had seven branches in the drawing. In life, it had nine.

Or left the notice posted and returned to her sleeping alcove, which sat behind the House’s second stair and was barely wide enough for a cot. She found the flour sack beneath her mattress, shook it empty, and laid the jar inside. She had brought it out of the Room Below Mouth because the jar was still making a sound. Now that she was alone, she could hear it clearly: taps in groups, separated by a pause. It was not a random patter. Someone was counting.

She put a bowl beside the jar and waited. The sound stopped whenever she leaned close. She backed into the doorway, holding her breath. The taps resumed. One, two, three. Then a long pause. Then one. She thought of the old system of weather marks: a vertical line for a day, a dot for a passing shower, a circle for an entire season. She had learned to draw them from her mother, who had taught her when she was small enough to sit on the floor beneath the inventory table.

Her mother’s hand had smelled of red dye and warm clay. She remembered that more clearly than her mother’s face. The memory was so small and ordinary that Or had never included it when she told people about losing her. The town used grand words for death and disappearance, but the things that stayed were often the smell of someone’s sleeves, or the way they counted the stairs out loud.

The jar tapped again. Or drew a little mark for every sound. After the fourth, the sequence repeated. She made four marks, then a space, then a fifth mark beneath the others. The shape looked like a map of the lower room: shelves along both walls and a narrow aisle between. The final tap came from somewhere at the base.

She returned to the Room Below Mouth after the House’s closing bell. She took no lamp. She knew the shelves by memory and could walk them without touching the vessels. At the lowest shelf, the jar’s place was bare. A thin line of damp ran down the stone beneath it, though the jars around it were dry.

She crouched and felt the base of the shelf. A small round groove circled a knot in the stone. The brass tide spoon fit the groove perfectly. Or pressed the crescent into it and turned. A square panel in the floor lifted at one corner, just enough to reveal a hollow beneath the shelf. No room lay below it, only a strip of blackness and a smell of wet soil.

She did not reach in. She had spent years learning how to be curious without being foolish. Instead, she covered the opening and placed the jar on the shelf above it. The tapping ceased. For a few seconds the House was utterly quiet.

Then, from somewhere in the walls, a bell rang once. It was not one of the orchard bells. It sounded like a cup being set down in an empty kitchen.

Or climbed back to her alcove. She wrote the day’s events on a scrap from her notebook, omitting Nemi’s name, Cale’s claim, and the blue wax. She wrote only: “The jar is not empty.” She folded the scrap and put it under her mattress. A clerk who wrote down every thought would soon have no thoughts left to herself.

In the morning, she would discover that the notice had curled inward. For now, she lay awake, listening to the House’s ordinary sounds: the pipes shrinking in the cool, the shelves settling, the distant breath of dry clouds. Somewhere below her, something counted one more drop.

Before dawn, Or climbed to the western roof and found the clouds had shifted. They lay low over the town, not above it, as if the sky had been poured into a basin. The roof tiles showed a thin green line leading toward the market court. Or traced it with her finger. The tile was not wet, but her fingertip came away cool. At the roof’s edge, she could see the Bell Orchard as a darker shape against the salt. One branch seemed to move though there was no wind. She wondered whether the town’s weather was not missing at all, only passing underneath them.

On her way down, she passed the roof tender’s small room. Pell was asleep in a chair with a brush still in his hand. A row of tiles beside him had turned from gray to green, one after another, as if the weather were walking across the roof. Or counted eight marks before the color stopped. She did not wake him. She wrote the number on the back of her hand and carried it downstairs.

The roof’s eight green marks faded by breakfast. Or wrote them in her notebook and left the page open beside the jar’s tally. She wondered whether the color had meant eight drops, eight turns, or eight names. The House offered no answer. Its pipes clicked in the walls, and the old jar remained silent on its shelf.

She closed the window. On the sill, an old measuring cup she kept for work held a single drop, clear as glass. It vanished when she blinked.
