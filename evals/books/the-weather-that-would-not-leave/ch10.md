# Chapter 10: The Place to Pass Through

Or met Iven in Low Weather. He had written a list of names on the green glass roof above the courtyard. The letters showed from below as dark shadows: Nemi Tallow, Cale Rusk, Vael Senn, Mara Senn, Iven Orri, Oruva Senn. At the bottom, he had written Vey Orin.

“Why are they there?” Or asked.

“People are forgetting themselves. I wanted something the sky could see.”

“Will it work?”

“It has not yet failed.”

Or set Iven’s green flask on the floor. The black line inside pointed west. “Mara says the fourth name is a direction.”

“West?”

“Down.”

Iven studied the green glass. “The storm needs a path through town. We sealed the path, then blamed the storm for being trapped.”

“Where does it go?”

“The Red Channel.”

“The channel is dry.”

“Because the town took its rain and gave it to the cistern.”

Or took the old map from Vael’s collar when the clerk came into the courtyard. Vael was too startled to stop her. The marked line ran from the Bell Orchard down through the under-room, then along the Red Channel to the old dye-house. The dye-house sat where the channel bent west; the red water there had once flowed out into the market basin beneath what was now the market court.

“Why did you say the first drop was a trick of the lanterns?” Or asked Vael.

“I wanted people to stay calm.”

“The rain did not fall on the orchard. It fell around it.”

“I know.”

“Then the statement was false.”

“Yes.” Vael’s face seemed older under the green glass light. “I was wrong to say it.”

“The old claim that the orchard was stealing rain was wrong too.”

“The roots were touching the cistern. I thought they were drinking.”

“They were holding the tree flask.”

“I know that now.”

Vael took the map back and folded it inside her collar. She had believed for most of Or’s life that the Bell Orchard had stolen rain from the town. Now she understood that the tree was a lid, not a thief.

Cale arrived from the island carrying the broken branch. The bells on it had started to ring, faintly. “The tree is opening,” he said. “The tree flask is needed beneath it.”

Mara came out of the courtyard’s shadow. She had her hood down. Vael’s hands tightened around the map.

“You let her in?” Vael asked Or.

“No. She was already here.”

Mara gave Vael the copper plate from her thumb. It was scratched with a mark shaped like a hooked letter. “You can keep this. I don’t need it anymore.”

Vael took the plate, then looked at Mara. “I’m sorry I believed the ledger.”

“I’m sorry you thought it was safer to believe it.”

Before they left the courtyard, Vael went back to the Counting Room and removed the labels from the empty jars. She did not throw them away. She sorted them into three piles: labels she could still read, labels that had faded, and labels that had been scraped clean. The last pile was the largest.

“What are you doing?” Or asked.

“Trying to see what we have chosen to forget.”

Vael set a scraped label beside the old map. It bore a pressure mark, a word that could be read only when light crossed it from the side. Or tilted the paper. “Passage,” she read.

Another label said “Witness.” A third said “Return.” The words had not been written as weather names. They were instructions, stored in the vessels that had once carried the storm’s parts. Vael sorted them by the depth of their creases and found a sequence: Witness, Passage, Return.

“What does the last one mean?” Iven asked.

“I don’t know,” Vael said. “Perhaps the storm returns.”

“Or the rain returns,” Or said.

Iven brought the blue bowl from the Counting Room, carrying it between the ledger and the map. The labels were placed in the blue bowl. The words became visible from beneath the glass. When they were arranged in the proper order, the bowl’s bottom showed a line pointing west. The same direction appeared on Iven’s green flask.

Iven took the old ledger and read the first bargain aloud. The wording was simple: “The storm may pass through, and the town will not hold its name.” The last clause had been crossed out and amended to read, “The town will hold its name.” Someone had changed the agreement after the first storm.

“Who changed it?” Or asked.

Vael examined the ink. “My predecessor.”

“Then the town broke the bargain.”

“Someone did.”

“Your office did.”

Vael’s hands tightened around the ledger. “Yes.”

She did not defend the decision. She said the earlier clerks feared that if the storm passed through, the town would lose its name and all its people would forget where they belonged. They had altered the words to keep Ors intact. Over time, the altered version became the only one anyone knew.

“Was their fear reasonable?” Or asked.

“No one knows. We never let the storm pass.”

“Then we can’t know until we try.”

“That is what frightens me.”

Vael put the original bargain ledger into a waterproof wrap and handed it to Iven. He would carry it to the island. The list of names went to Or. Pell’s tile chart went to Cale. Each person carried a piece of the route, not because the objects needed to be divided, but because no single person should hold the whole record again.



They walked together toward the Bell Orchard. Before leaving, Or retrieved the first jar from the Room Below Mouth shelf and carried it with her. Iven carried the blue tooth. Nemi joined them at the causeway, with her six-lidded box in one hand. Or handed her the tin case she had carried since the House. Or gave her the red-thread vial, now holding the remaining rain, and Nemi tied it to her apron.

They paused at the edge of the causeway before crossing. The waterless channel was visible below through a gap in the stone, its old course a dark seam under the town. Nemi said she had once thought a container made a thing safer by keeping it close. Or looked at the tin case she was returning and said that safety could also mean leaving room for something to go where it was meant to go. Neither of them called that a lesson. They had both learned it by losing things first.

Vael walked at the back of the group. She carried the copper plate Mara had given her, wrapped in a scrap of ledger cloth. She asked Mara whether the mark on it had been made before the bargain was changed. Mara said she did not know. Vael accepted the answer without opening the cloth or asking for a better one. For once, a record did not have to be complete before it could be trusted.

At the island, the tree roots had drawn back from the earth. The tree flask beneath the bark showed green and clear. Cale lifted the broken branch and set it against the trunk. Its bells rang once, twice, then fell silent.

Or took the red-thread vial from Nemi and poured its contents onto the soil. Nothing happened. She returned the empty vial to Nemi. Or gave the black seed back to Nemi, who tucked it beneath the sixth lid of her box. Nemi opened her six-lidded box and removed the pinch of white salt. She scattered it over the wet earth. The salt took the shape of letters.

“Now,” said Vey Orin.

She stood beneath the tree, no longer hidden by her scarf. Or had thought the copper hand belonged to a stranger. It belonged to the first Listener, who had been returned to the town without being returned to its ledger.

Vey spoke her name. “Vey Orin.”

Mara said, “Mara Senn.”

Or felt the third name rise from the first jar’s crack, which she had brought from the House. “Oruva Senn.”

The ground shivered. But the tree did not open.

Iven listened. “It wants the place.”

Or looked west. “The Red Channel.”

The storm’s name was not a word. It was the direction of a path left unfinished. It wanted a way through, not a jar to live in. Or had thought the blue tooth would open the tree flask and release a flood. Instead, it was a key for turning the vessel toward its door.

Cale pushed the branch against the tree. The bells gave a low note. The tree flask loosened in the bark. Cale drew the flask free and left it beside the oldest root, then took the branch back under his arm. Or still held Iven’s green flask, its black line pointing west.

“Take Iven’s green flask with the black line to the channel,” Vey said. “Let the storm pass through.”

 They were afraid of different outcomes. Cale feared the tree would die. Nemi feared the storm would be trapped again. Iven feared he would fail to hear the answer. Vael feared the town would forget itself. Or feared that everyone would ask her to choose alone.

She told them she would not. They would carry Iven’s green flask together and let the path answer.

The group paused at the causeway while Cale and Vael argued over what the storm might do. Cale said the oldest tree could not survive another winter without its missing branch. Vael said the town could not risk losing its stored weather. Nemi said the stored weather was already gone. Iven said none of them could know the outcome.

Or listened until they stopped speaking. “We can open a path without deciding what the storm will do after it leaves.”

Vael said the town had to know whether the rain would return. Or replied that the town had no right to make the storm promise that. The words came out more sharply than she intended. She softened them by adding that the people could ask, but they could not force an answer.

Or took the copper ring from her coat pocket and gave it to Mara. Mara held it. Vey stood beside her. They did not agree on whether the town should apologize to the storm. Vey said a storm could not receive an apology. Mara said the people needed to make one anyway. Neither tried to settle the question.

They left the orchard by the causeway, carrying the storm’s vessel and the record of the bargain together. The path through town was still closed in places, and the black line in Iven’s flask would have to guide them one threshold at a time. Cale kept the broken branch under his arm as they walked. He would lay it at the cistern mouth when they reached it.

Mara and Vey followed behind Or. They had both spent years being spoken of by other people, in records that made them sound like a warning. Now they walked under their own names. Vael carried no notice; Iven kept the wrapped original ledger. Or held the list of the three Listeners, folded small enough to fit in her palm. She thought of how easily a name could be made to look like a destination. The route would have to remain open after they passed, or the town would have learned nothing from letting the storm go.

At the town end of the causeway, they stopped only long enough for the orchard bells to settle. Their faint note followed them toward the Red Channel. No one claimed to know what the storm would leave behind. They had agreed to find out together.
